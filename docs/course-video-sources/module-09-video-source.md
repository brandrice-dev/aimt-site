## What the module is really about

Module 8 ends deliberately at the treatment close, with one approved
closing line ("Today I focused a little more on [area or service
priority you addressed]. How are you feeling?"). Module 9 teaches
everything that happens after that moment: reorienting the client,
closing the appointment without breaking the relaxation experience the
whole course has built toward, and pricing the business from real
numbers rather than pressure, guesswork, or copying a competitor.
`module-09.md` names this directly: **"Module 9 is a Business Decision
Lab, not a sales lesson. It should feel like the module where the
student stops performing a script and starts reasoning like a business
owner."**

This is not a script-memorization or sales-technique module. It teaches
a five-part closing *shape* (reorient → briefly recap → answer/recommend
where relevant → invite future options without pressure → complete
checkout) rather than a fixed script, and a pricing *method* (real cost
+ full practitioner time + a deliberately chosen margin) rather than a
single prescribed number. The video must preserve that framing: this
module hands the student a way of thinking, not a formula to recite or
a benchmark to copy.

## Approved outcomes

Drawn from `module-09.md`, "Approved outcomes." Prioritized for an
opening video:

- Transition from treatment closing into checkout without abruptly
  shifting into sales mode.
- Communicate what was done during the service without diagnosing or
  exaggerating outcomes.
- Invite future booking or service options without pressure.
- Identify the actual cost components required to deliver a Head Spa
  service, including full practitioner time — not treatment time alone.
- Calculate a target selling price from a chosen margin without
  confusing margin, markup, and break-even.
- Use competitor pricing as market context rather than the primary
  pricing formula.
- Design a concise, meaningfully differentiated service menu.
- Respond professionally when a client says the price felt high.
- Distinguish among a cost problem, pricing problem, menu problem,
  positioning problem, delivery problem, or market-fit problem before
  deciding what to change.

## Central practitioner payoff

Verified against `module-09.md`'s "Signature learning moment" section.
The payoff is not a memorized script or a single "correct" price — it
is the shift from **"What are other people charging?"** to **"What does
this actually cost me to deliver, what price supports the business, and
can the client understand what they're paying for?"** Compressed into
specific practitioner truths:

- A confident close does not require a sales script — it requires a
  repeatable shape (reorient, recap, answer, invite, checkout) the
  practitioner can trust under any outcome, including a client who
  declines everything.
- "Full practitioner time" is broader than the clock spent hands-on —
  setup, consultation, transitions, cleanup, checkout, and
  documentation all belong in a service's real cost, not just the
  treatment minutes.
- Margin and markup are not the same number from the same starting
  cost — confusing them silently changes the practitioner's actual
  price.
- One client saying a price "felt high" is one data point, not a
  verdict — it can mean several different things, including nothing
  that needs to change at all.
- The mistake this knowledge prevents: pricing reactively — copying a
  competitor's number, defaulting to an unexplained percentage, or
  discounting the moment a client pushes back — none of which the
  practitioner can actually defend with their own numbers.

## Beginner misconception or mistake corrected

Two related beginner habits, both directly named in `module-09.md`:
treating the post-treatment moment as a sales pitch rather than a
continuation of the same relaxation-first experience the client has
been in for the whole appointment, and pricing reactively — copying a
competitor's number, accepting an unexplained default margin, or
assuming a single universal hourly benchmark — rather than building a
price from the practitioner's own real costs and a deliberately chosen
margin. A third, related misconception: assuming a client's "that felt
expensive" comment automatically proves the price (or the client) is
wrong, rather than treating it as one piece of business evidence to
review in context.

## Insider knowledge

Verified against `module-09.md`'s section-by-section "Key teaching
points" (see also "Central practitioner payoff" above — do not
duplicate the full list on screen; use it to select the strongest one
or two points):

- Margin (calculated from selling price) and markup (calculated from
  cost) produce different prices from the same starting numbers — a
  distinction most new practitioners have never been taught before
  they need it.
- A client's price pushback is genuinely ambiguous evidence — it can
  reflect a cost problem, a pricing problem, a menu-clarity problem, a
  positioning problem, a delivery mismatch, a market-fit issue, or
  simply one client's preference. Reacting with an immediate discount
  skips the actual diagnosis.
- Underpricing has several real causes beyond fear or low confidence —
  incomplete cost data, poor overhead allocation, inaccurate time
  assumptions, competitor copying, or an intentional launch strategy
  among them. Confidence is not a financial model.

## Approved learning rhythm

Module 9 is **decision-led**, distinct from Module 8's technique-led,
video-masterclass rhythm. Its dominant learning mode is applying a
numeric/business framework to the student's own real or planned
business, and rehearsing a specific social moment (price pushback)
under calm, non-defensive judgment. Its approved interaction density is
light-to-moderate and deliberate — `module-09.md` is explicit that nothing
beyond the following exists or should exist:

1. **Close Without Pressure** (new, ungraded) — a realistic
   post-treatment scenario with several candidate closing responses;
   the student selects the one that best preserves the relaxation
   experience while moving toward checkout. Text-based feedback only;
   no score, no persistence, no completion gate.
2. **The Cost Base → Target Price calculator** — the module's signature
   applied tool; the student's own numbers produce three distinct
   labeled values (modeled cost base, selected target margin,
   calculated target price).
3. Two required checkpoints (`m10cp1` — menu/pricing reasoning;
   `m10cp2` — price-feedback and closing communication under pressure).

## Presenter emphasis

Confident, business-minded, practical, calm, direct, premium, and
instructor-led — the tone of someone who has priced and closed real
services many times, not a sales trainer. The student should come away
understanding that pricing and closing are business judgment calls
grounded in the student's own numbers and their own client relationship
— not a script to perform or a formula to copy. Avoid any register that
could read as motivational-speaker or "confidence hack" content; the
module's own governing language is explicit that confidence is not a
financial model.

## Video boundaries

**The opening video should:**

- establish the central transition named in the task brief: a great
  service still needs a business model that supports it — moving the
  student from service execution, to client closing, to business
  judgment;
- introduce client closing as a continuation of the relaxation-first
  experience, not a shift into sales mode;
- introduce pricing as something built from the practitioner's own real
  costs, full time, and a deliberately chosen margin — not competitor
  copying, fear, or guesswork;
- introduce the idea that a client's price pushback is business
  information to evaluate, not automatic proof the price or the client
  is wrong;
- preview that the student will learn to close intentionally and price
  from real numbers — without teaching the calculator's math, narrating
  every menu-design rule, or resolving either checkpoint's answer.

**The opening video should not:**

- narrate the full five-part closing shape as a script to memorize;
- teach the calculator's formula or walk through a full sample
  calculation;
- reveal which Close Without Pressure response is strongest, or show any
  distractor's feedback text;
- show `m10cp1` or `m10cp2`'s model answers or Cadence's evaluation of a
  live response;
- state or imply a specific dollar figure, hourly rate, or margin
  percentage as AIMT's recommendation;
- describe or characterize Module 10's (Sanitation) actual curriculum
  beyond naming it as what comes next;
- adopt a sales-guru, "close the sale," or revenue-hype tone at any
  point.

Interface footage may be captured because Module 9 is approved for
video production, but use it sparingly and keep interaction/checkpoint
solutions protected, per the Module 5/6/7 precedent.

## Suggested duration

**Approximately 90–120 seconds (1:30–2:00).** Module 9 has no existing
photographic or illustrative assets to draw on (unlike Modules 3, 4, 5,
and 7), and its own governing content direction is explicit that this
is orientation and curiosity, not full instruction — the calculator,
the full closing shape, and both checkpoints all remain deliberately
untaught on screen. A duration toward the lower half of the master
90-second–2:30 range is appropriate: enough to establish the
service-execution → client-closing → business-judgment transition, the
closing shape at a conceptual level, and the real-numbers pricing
reframe, without padding a text-forward module with manufactured visual
length.
