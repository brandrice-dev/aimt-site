## What the module is really about

Module 1 is the professional-boundary foundation for the entire course. It
draws one central line — observation versus diagnosis — and builds
everything else around it: that "head spa technician" describes a role
performed during a service, not a separate state license; that scope
depends on the student's actual license and jurisdiction, not a universal
list; and that a head spa service has real, honest cosmetic benefits that
stop well short of treating or diagnosing disease.

## Approved outcomes

- Describe a head spa as a structured, scalp-focused cosmetic and wellness
  service rather than a medical treatment.
- Distinguish professional observation from medical diagnosis.
- Use client-facing language that describes visible findings without
  naming a medical condition.
- Recognize when a finding requires referral to a medical professional.
- Explain realistic service benefits and limitations without promising
  diagnosis, cure, or hair regrowth.
- Understand that legal scope depends on license, state law, and the
  service actually performed.

## Central practitioner payoff

Gives the student language they can actually use with a real client under
pressure — a client asking "do I have alopecia?" — without either
overclaiming (naming a diagnosis) or underdelivering (being unable to say
anything useful). It converts "stay in scope" from a vague warning into a
specific, rehearsable skill.

## Beginner misconception or mistake corrected

That a confident-sounding observation is the same as a safe one, and that
"within scope" is a fixed, universal list. The module corrects both:
observation must stay grounded in what's visible, and scope is
license-and-jurisdiction-dependent, not something the course itself can
grant.

## Insider knowledge

- The single most consequential mistake is turning a visible finding into a
  medical conclusion — recognizing a pattern can inform the cosmetic
  service, but naming it (dermatitis, alopecia, fungal) crosses into
  diagnosis regardless of how confident it sounds.
- Referral is not a professional failure — it's timed judgment. New,
  sudden, severe, persistent, spreading, painful, or bleeding findings are
  the trigger list, not a vague "when in doubt."
- Massage is honestly framed around relaxation and sensory value, not
  circulation-as-treatment — this protects the practitioner from a claim
  they can't actually support.
- The same head spa activity may be legal for one license type and
  prohibited for another; "within scope" language has to stay
  license-dependent, never universal.

## Approved learning rhythm

Structured around a scope-and-language sequence (definitions → safe/unsafe
client scripts → referral guidance → licensing) that builds toward one new
ungraded interaction — "Where is the line?," a four-scenario
observation-versus-outside-scope sort — placed between the scope cards and
the service-limitations section, immediately before the two required
checkpoints. Scope and safety framing is intentionally the dominant tone
here because scope *is* this module's subject — that emphasis is not meant
to generalize to later modules (see the video-direction file's note on
varied learning rhythm).

## Relationship to adjacent modules

Follows directly from the Welcome Module's "lead the experience" standard by
giving that leadership a boundary: leading still means staying inside
professional and legal scope. Its own approved completion copy hands off
explicitly: "With the professional boundary established, Module 2 moves
into the client experience before the scalp service begins: intake, first
contact, communication, and the opening moments that establish trust."

## Claims and language that must not be reintroduced

- "A healthier environment that supports hair growth" — replaced with
  cosmetic/comfort-only language; the module explicitly states a head spa
  service does not diagnose, treat, reverse, or regrow hair.
- "Massage & circulation — the therapeutic core of the service" — replaced
  with "Massage & relaxation — the sensory core of the service."
  Circulation is not a promised clinical outcome.
- Universal "within scope" claims — replaced with license-dependent framing
  ("may fall within scope — verify first").
- Flaking language that steers toward a named diagnosis (e.g., "consistent
  with dandruff").
- The old course names.
- Cadence claiming personal industry experience.

## Presenter emphasis

Direct and unflinching about the boundary — this module should feel like
the moment the course draws a hard, protective line, not a legal disclaimer
recited quickly to get past it. The instructor should frame scope discipline
as what actually builds client trust, not as a limitation on the
practitioner.

## Video boundaries

Preview the observation-versus-diagnosis distinction and the reality that
scope depends on the student's own license — do not narrate the full
safe-language script table, the four "Where is the line?" scenarios, or
either checkpoint question. Interface footage of the interaction and
checkpoints must wait for a recorded manual-QA step.
