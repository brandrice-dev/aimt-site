## What the module is really about

Module 5 is the course's turn from observation to decision. Module 4
taught the student how to gather and describe evidence without
overinterpreting it; Module 5 teaches what to responsibly do with that
evidence. Its central move is replacing the instinct to force an entire
scalp into one fixed "type" with a repeatable decision model: read the
current regional presentation, weigh what the client reports and their
recent history, identify the safest service limit, and only then decide
what to adjust — cleansing, exfoliation, water and steam, pressure and
tempo, or product placement — region by region. The module's own approved
framing states it plainly: "The goal is not to assign one permanent
scalp type. The goal is to make each step earn its place."

## Approved outcomes

Drawn from `module-05.md`, "Approved outcomes" (verify exact numbering
against the source before final scripting). Prioritized for an opening
video:

- Recognize that different regions of the same scalp may present
  differently, and that one dominant feature should not govern the whole
  service.
- Combine visible observation with client-reported history (wash timing,
  recent products, chemical services, heat exposure, symptoms, reactions)
  to interpret what the image alone cannot establish.
- Translate that combined evidence into a service decision across five
  levers: cleansing, exfoliation, water and steam, pressure and tempo, and
  product placement and finish.
- Adjust service intensity and product placement regionally rather than
  uniformly.
- Distinguish a supported cosmetic pattern description (baseline/
  maintenance, oil-dominant or residue-present, fine-scale/dry-appearing,
  mixed regional, reactive/sensitivity-reported) from an unsupported
  diagnosis.
- Explain a service decision — what was observed, what changed the
  interpretation, what was modified, what was intentionally left out — in
  calm, non-alarming client-facing language.
- Preserve a stable presentation instead of adding unnecessary corrective
  steps, and know when to modify, avoid, pause, or refer per Module 4's
  framework.

## Central practitioner payoff

Module 5 makes the student a better service designer, not a better
protocol-selector. The payoff is less trial and error (a repeatable
four-question model replaces guessing), more intentional customization
(the same appointment can responsibly treat the crown differently than
the hairline without feeling fragmented to the client), stronger regional
decision-making, and — critically — the ability to explain *why* a
service choice was made rather than simply performing steps. The module
also teaches restraint as a professional skill: knowing when *not* to add
a stronger step, because "the safest limit outranks the client's
request," is presented as more advanced practice than reaching for more
product, more pressure, or more time.

## Beginner misconception or mistake corrected

The central weak habit Module 5 overturns: **assuming the entire scalp
needs one label, one protocol, or one intensity** — reading one feature,
assigning one type, and running the identical protocol everywhere.
Section 5.9 names eight related mistakes, each paired with an approved
"Better move" correction (do not reproduce the full lesson in the video;
name the pattern, not all eight in detail):

1. **Label first** — categorizing before gathering regional evidence.
2. **Product first** — choosing the product before defining the need.
3. **Whole scalp, one intensity** — applying the strongest regional need
   everywhere.
4. **Exfoliating every flake** — treating visible scale as automatic
   permission to exfoliate.
5. **Strongest means best** — assuming maximum steam, pressure, or
   product creates a more valuable service.
6. **Image becomes diagnosis** — turning a visible feature into a medical
   conclusion.
7. **Client request becomes permission** — continuing an inappropriate
   step because the client asked for it.
8. **Improvement becomes cure** — treating a more comfortable or
   cleaner-looking result as proof a condition was treated.

## Insider knowledge

Verified against `module-05.md`, "Insider value and acceleration payoff":

- The safest limiting factor should be identified *before* the treatment
  goal — limit first, priority second, region by region.
- The same scalp can require different intensity, product placement, or
  omission by region in the same appointment.
- A premium service is not defined by performing every available step.
- Product selection should follow the service decision, not create it —
  "the right product is the one that supports the decision you already
  made."
- Client-reported burning or tenderness can outweigh a visually minor
  presentation.
- Visible scale is not automatic permission to exfoliate.
- Dry shampoo and styling residue can create a different service decision
  than oil alone.
- A complete service can remain cohesive even when the protocol changes
  region by region — regional customization should feel intentional, not
  improvised or fragmented to the client.
- Client trust is protected by explaining the reason for a modification,
  not by performing an inappropriate requested step.

## Approved learning rhythm

Module 5 is decision-led where Module 4 was observation-led: Module 4's
job was to collect views, describe what is visible, and separate evidence
from conclusion; Module 5's job is to decide what should responsibly
change in the service because of those observations. The approved
rhythm moves from a short conceptual reset ("a protocol is a decision,
not a label") through the five service levers and the safety/priority
order, into the five common service-direction patterns, then the
module's signature ungraded interaction — **"What changes first?"**, a
four-scenario decision exercise with choice-specific feedback and no
automatic answer reveal — before the regional protocol builder and
midpoint checkpoint (`m5cp1`). The second half covers product-category
decisions, intensity (steam/water/pressure/time), and client-communication
scripts, closes with Section 5.9's eight mistake → "Better move" pairs,
and ends at the final checkpoint (`m5cp2`) followed directly by the
competency-based completion card. Both checkpoints require independent
written reasoning, not selection from options.

## Claims and language that must not be reintroduced

Verified against `module-05.md`, "Required corrections":

- The fixed five-scalp-type framing (neutral/oily/dry/combination/
  sensitive) as permanent, mutually exclusive client identities.
- Unsupported compensatory-oil claims (that over-cleansing automatically
  causes rebound oil production).
- Follicular-obstruction or "clogged follicle" conclusions drawn from
  appearance alone.
- Unsupported hair-growth claims (visible material or barrier condition
  impairing or restoring hair growth).
- Universal baseline scalp-color/translucency claims (a healthy scalp
  must show a particular tone, sheen, or translucency).
- Unsupported percentage claims (e.g., a fixed sebum-increase percentage
  per temperature change, or the removed "60 to 90 percent of follicles"
  dry-scalp criterion).
- Diet- or vitamin-based diagnostic conclusions presented as a scalp
  protocol.
- Diagnostic language generally — a magnified or photographed appearance
  proving sensitivity, inflammation, infection, dehydration, or a barrier
  disorder.
- Implying visible flakes/scale automatically require exfoliation.
- Implying stronger intensity (steam, pressure, product, time) is
  inherently a better or more valuable service.
- Implying a cleaner appearance or more comfortable service proves a
  condition was treated or cured.
- Treating any of the five illustrative teaching photographs — especially
  the consultation image's background prop graphic — as authenticated
  clinical evidence, real documentation, or official AIMT terminology.
- Named-condition teaching (dandruff, Malassezia, seborrheic dermatitis)
  — that content belongs to the still-unaudited Module 6, not Module 5.
- The old course name ("HeadSpa Mastery") or any claim that Cadence holds
  personal practitioner experience.

## Presenter emphasis

The instructor should make Module 5 feel like the transition from
*seeing* to *deciding*. The tone communicates professional confidence
through judgment and restraint, not through disclaimers. The student
should come away understanding that customization is neither random
creativity nor simply adding more product or intensity — it is the
disciplined ability to connect observation → context → service decision
without exceeding what the evidence supports. Keep this elevated and
practitioner-centered; scope and safety language belongs where a specific
decision requires it (the safety-limit framing, the reactive/sensitivity
response), not as the video's dominant mood.

## Video boundaries

The opening video should preview: why one uniform scalp label is often
too crude for real service planning; why regional differences matter;
why the practitioner needs client context before changing the service;
and how professional customization comes from reasoned decisions, not
performing every available step. Leave the full lesson to teach the
complete five-lever framework, all five service-direction patterns, all
eight mistake/"Better move" pairs, the full four-scenario "What changes
first?" interaction, and both checkpoints' full reasoning. Do not reveal
any scenario's approved answer from "What changes first?" or narrate
Sections 5.1–5.9 in sequence. Interface footage may be captured because
Module 5 is approved for video production, but use it sparingly — one or
two deliberate moments, not a screen-recording walkthrough.

## Suggested duration

**120–150 seconds.** Module 5 sits in the middle of the master 90-second–
2:30 range: it is more conceptually dense than the earlier, more linear
modules (it has to land a decision *model*, not just a single insight),
but it does not carry the same volume of new technical vocabulary as
Module 3 or the procedural density of Module 4's five-point scan. Five
approved teaching images support a visually varied 120–150 seconds
without needing to be stretched to fill a longer runway.
