## What the module is really about

Module 10 is not "Sanitation 101." Its audience is primarily
**already-licensed cosmetologists and estheticians** who completed
state-required sanitation/disinfection education before ever reaching this
course. The module teaches how those already-known fundamentals apply to
the specific realities of a Head Spa environment — wet systems, halo/water
equipment, reusable tools, service surfaces, linens, product handling, and
room turnover — and, critically, how a practitioner keeps that standard
intact across an entire operating day, not only in the narrow window
between two clients.

The opening video's job is to correct a scope assumption before the lesson
begins: sanitation is not a between-clients task. It is a continuous
operating system that has to be built before the first client arrives, held
during the working day, executed between every service, and closed out
after the last client leaves — with equipment and wet-system care running
underneath all of it. `module-10.md`'s own approved framing states this
directly: *"You already know sanitation fundamentals. This module teaches
you how to apply them to the specific realities of a Head Spa."*

## Approved outcomes

Drawn from `module-10.md`, "Approved outcomes." Prioritized for an opening
video:

- Distinguish cleaning, disinfection, laundering/replacement/disposal, and
  reset as different actions, not interchangeable words.
- Apply the correct general processing category to common Head Spa items
  (tools, surfaces, linens, single-use items, halo/water equipment,
  product handling).
- Explain why disinfectant labels and equipment instructions — not a
  memorized AIMT number — control product- and equipment-specific
  procedure.
- Build a repeatable reset that never shortens a genuinely required
  process/contact time.
- Keep clean and used items separated throughout the day.
- Recognize when a normal reset is insufficient and an incident/exposure
  procedure is required instead.
- Build a room-specific system that still holds up when the schedule is
  under pressure.

## Central practitioner payoff

Verified against `module-10.md`'s core thesis and "Module 10 core
direction — locked" section. The payoff is not a faster reset — it is the
shift from **"How fast can I turn this room over?"** to **"What does this
room and equipment actually require before it's genuinely ready again?"**
Compressed into specific practitioner truths:

- A strong system reduces decisions without reducing standards — the goal
  is never simply "move faster."
- The room, tools, and equipment don't only need attention between
  clients; readiness has to exist before the first client, hold through
  the day, and close out properly at the end of it.
- Efficiency is a *result* of a correct system, practiced until it's
  repeatable — never a shortcut taken because the schedule is tight.
- The strongest test of a system isn't how it performs on an easy day —
  it's whether it still holds up when the practitioner is behind.

---

## Beginner misconception or mistake corrected

The habit of framing professional reset as a speed problem —
**"How fast can I turn this room over?"** — rather than a completeness
problem — **"What does this room and equipment actually require before it
is ready again?"** A related, narrower misconception the module corrects:
treating sanitation as something that only happens *between* clients,
rather than a system that also has to be built before the first client and
closed out after the last one.

---

## Insider knowledge

Verified against `module-10.md`'s section-by-section content:

- Cleaning, disinfection, laundering/replacement/disposal, reset, and
  sterilization are five different actions — using the wrong one in the
  wrong place, not simply "not cleaning enough," is where real risk lives.
- An ITEM → PROCESS lens (what does *this specific item* actually require)
  replaces a one-size-fits-all frequency schedule that a course can't
  honestly certify for every setup.
- A system that "reduces decisions without reducing standards" — known
  clean/used zones, known label/contact-time instructions, accessible
  equipment instructions, clean backups, a room-specific reset order — is
  what actually survives a bad day, not last-minute discipline.
- Records exist for consistency, maintenance history, and traceability —
  not as an automatic legal shield.

## Approved learning rhythm

Module 10's density is **light-to-moderate and deliberate**, distinct from
Module 9's business-decision rhythm and Module 8's masterclass-video
rhythm. The module is framed as an **Operations Pressure Test.** Per
`module-10.md`, nothing beyond the following exists or should exist:

1. **Reset Under Pressure** (new, ungraded) — a realistic scenario (the
   next client has arrived early while a required disinfectant contact
   time is still running); the student selects the response that best
   preserves the required process. Text-based feedback only; no score, no
   persistence, no completion gate.
2. Two required checkpoints (`m9cp1` — between-client reset reasoning;
   `m9cp2` — post-service concern response).

## Video boundaries

**The opening video should:**

- establish that a professional sanitation/readiness system operates
  across the entire service day — before the first client, throughout the
  day, between services, after the last client, and through ongoing
  equipment/wet-system care — not only "between clients";
- open on a strong operational thought, e.g. *"A clean room is not
  something you create once. It is a system you maintain all day"*;
- show the all-day operating rhythm visually (Before Opening → During the
  Day → Between Services → End of Day);
- introduce the ITEM → PROCESS and Contain → Clean → Disinfect/Process →
  Reset → Verify frameworks at a conceptual, glimpse level;
- preview the Reset Under Pressure premise (a system tested by an early
  client) without resolving which response is strongest;
- mention, briefly and without becoming a disclaimer, that exact
  procedures depend on the practitioner's applicable requirements, product
  directions, and equipment instructions;
- preview that the student will learn to build a system that holds up
  under real schedule pressure — without teaching the module's actual
  process categories, contact-time specifics, or either checkpoint's
  answer.

**The opening video should not:**

- teach detailed disinfectant chemistry, dilution, product-specific
  contact times, BARBICIDE procedures, halo-line chemical recipes,
  state-specific regulation, equipment-specific cleaning instructions, or
  blood/body-fluid procedure detail;
- present any AIMT procedure as a universal legal requirement;
- reveal `m9cp1` or `m9cp2`'s model answers or Cadence's evaluation of a
  live response;
- reveal which Reset Under Pressure response is strongest, or show any
  distractor's feedback text;
- read or list the downloadable checklist's interior fields on screen;
- describe or characterize Module 11's actual curriculum beyond naming it
  as what comes next;
- adopt an alarmist, preachy, or remedial ("Sanitation 101") tone at any
  point;
- become a montage of spraying and wiping surfaces.

Interface footage may be captured because Module 10 is approved for video
production, but use it sparingly and keep the interaction/checkpoint
solutions protected, per the Module 9 precedent.

## Production flags

- Beyond the newly installed downloadable, Module 10 has no existing
  photography, diagram, or illustration assets — any visual richness in
  the video comes from new B-roll, restrained typography, or brief
  interface footage, not from an existing image library the way Modules
  3–5 and 7 have.
- **Text-callout accuracy flag:** a shortened callout form like "Clean →
  Process → Reset → Verify" would compress the approved five-phase
  framework by dropping "Contain" and merging "Disinfect" into "Process."
  The master video instructions prohibit silently changing approved
  terminology — use the full, accurate "Contain → Clean → Disinfect/
  Process → Reset → Verify" sequence on screen (per "Approved text-callout
  opportunities" above) rather than a compressed four-word version, even
  though the longer form takes slightly more screen time.
- Blood/body-fluid incident-response content (Section 10.5) must not be
  dramatized, detailed, or visually referenced beyond an abstract mention
  that routine reset sometimes isn't enough — the video boundaries above
  exclude procedural detail here entirely.
- Live-model checkpoint grading QA (`m9cp1`/`m9cp2`) remains deferred —
  verified only by rubric/config inspection and mocked validation, not by
  exercising the real model against live student answers.
- Live Cadence response QA remains deferred — quick-prompt text and the
  guide-system prompt were verified by source inspection only.
- Screen-reader QA, physical-keyboard QA on real hardware, and real
  touch-device QA all remain deferred.
- None of the above deferred items may become implied claims in the
  video (e.g., do not imply grading has been verified against a live
  model, or that accessibility testing is complete).
- This module explicitly teaches a decision framework, not a specific
  product, brand, or jurisdiction's exact rules — the video must not
  improvise any of these during filming.

## Suggested duration

**Approximately 75–105 seconds.** This is deliberately shorter than the
90-second–2:30 master default: Module 10 does not need a long opening. It
should establish the professional, full-workday-system mindset, show the
all-day operating rhythm visually, and move the student into the lesson —
without teaching the item/process categories, the five-phase framework in
detail, the Reset Under Pressure scenario's resolution, or either
checkpoint's content.
