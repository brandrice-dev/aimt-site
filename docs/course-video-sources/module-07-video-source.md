## What the module is really about

Module 5 taught the student to adapt a service; Module 6 taught the
student to interpret what they're looking at. Module 7 teaches the
student to build the physical and procedural system the rest of the
course happens inside — not by listing equipment, but by teaching what a
setup needs to accomplish (access, comfort, safety, consistency, speed)
and what happens when it doesn't. The module's own module-opening
framing states the core idea directly: "Equipment and room setup feel
like logistics, but they directly shape the service. A well-designed
station removes friction before the first step even begins."

This is a module about **function and practitioner judgment, not
equipment shopping.** It does not endorse one bed model, one room
layout, or one "correct" way to arrange a station — it teaches the
evaluation criteria a student can apply to whatever setup they're
actually offered or can afford. The video must preserve that framing:
different setups can both be correct if they both perform their
function; a setup is judged by what it supports (positioning, reach,
service flow, comfort, readiness), never by how closely it copies one
treatment room or how polished it looks.

## Approved outcomes

Drawn from `module-07.md`, "Approved outcomes." Prioritized for an
opening video:

- Evaluate a head spa bed against function-based decision categories
  (support, access, water management, stability, cleaning compatibility,
  space) rather than by appearance — and correctly identify when a
  feature is a strong preference versus a hard requirement.
- Arrange tools and products on a cart or station by service sequence and
  frequency of use — not by category alone — reducing mid-service
  reaching, backtracking, and stepping away.
- Position a client using three concrete physical checks (halo alignment,
  shoulder position, occipital support) and correctly identify when one
  of the three is wrong before water starts.
- Recognize the observable signs that a setup is not ready or a
  positioning is incorrect — in someone else's station, not only their
  own.
- State why sustained neck hyperextension against a hard basin edge is
  avoided, and what to watch for and do if a client reports strain,
  dizziness, or discomfort mid-service.
- Distinguish equipment and tools that are required for a professional
  head spa service from those that are useful upgrades or genuinely
  optional.

## Central practitioner payoff

Verified against `module-07.md`, "Practitioner insider value" and
"Insider value and acceleration payoff." The payoff is not a shopping
list or a single "correct" room — it is a repeatable evaluation
framework (for equipment, cart organization, and positioning) that
otherwise takes months of trial, backtracking, and client discomfort to
accumulate on the job. Compressed into specific practitioner truths:

- A setup that looks good can still create poor reach — an attractively
  arranged cart organized by category rather than sequence forces
  backtracking mid-service, invisible to the client but felt by the
  practitioner every appointment.
- A "comfortable-looking" bed can still leave the neck unsupported —
  visual comfort (padding, upholstery) is not the same as functional
  support (occipital curve, neutral angle).
- Positioned "close enough" is not positioned correctly — a few inches of
  shoulder-position error, or a slightly lifted chin, is invisible to a
  casual glance but is exactly the difference between a supported and an
  unsupported neck.
- More equipment can create more friction, not more capability — every
  upgrade-tier item added to the working surface is one more thing to
  reach past.
- The mistake this knowledge prevents: a practitioner who has to stop and
  improvise mid-service because something wasn't within reach, wasn't
  sanitized in time, or wasn't checked before the water started — all
  avoidable with a system built in advance, not skill applied in the
  moment.

## Beginner misconception or mistake corrected

Two related beginner habits, both named directly in `module-07.md`:
conflating how a setup *looks* with how it *functions* (an
attractive-but-poorly-organized cart, a comfortable-looking-but-
unsupportive bed), and building the working system reactively —
assembling comfort or ambient elements while the client is already
present, or discovering mid-service that something needed isn't within
reach — rather than running the same deliberate sequence every time.
Both stem from the same underlying error: treating setup as decoration
rather than as a system that determines how the service actually goes.

## Insider knowledge

Verified against `module-07.md`, "Practitioner insider value" (see also
"Central practitioner payoff" above — do not duplicate the full list
on screen; use it to select the strongest one or two points):

- Reach-zone cart organization is about frequency of use and service
  sequence, not a fixed room layout — the difference between a cart on
  the left versus the right is irrelevant if everything stays reachable.
- Prep steps get forgotten when they're staged reactively rather than run
  the same way every time — the value of a fixed sequence is error
  prevention, not ritual.
- The three positioning checks (halo alignment, shoulder position,
  occipital support) are functional access/support requirements, not
  practitioner preference — what *is* preference is how a practitioner
  physically executes the adjustment.

## Approved learning rhythm

Module 7 is **system-led** — distinct from Module 5's decision-led
rhythm and Module 6's interpretation-led rhythm. Its dominant learning
mode is applying a small number of durable evaluation frameworks
(function-based bed evaluation, reach-zone cart organization, three-point
positioning check) to a physical, procedural setup. Its final approved
interaction set is intentionally light-to-moderate, appropriate to
procedural subject matter:

1. **Tool-category disclosure** (`.tool-category`) — an accessible
   content-organization control, explicitly **not** a graded learning
   interaction. Do not present it on screen as if it teaches a
   judgment call.
2. **Station prep checklist** (`.prep-item`) — ungraded self-directed
   practice; resets on every reopen by design (this is correct behavior,
   not a defect).
3. **"Find the setup mistakes"** (signature interaction, new) — four
   visual judgment cards shown one at a time, each asking: "Does this
   setup need to change, or is it simply a different way of working?"
   with two choices, **Needs correction** and **Acceptable variation**.
   This is the module's real practitioner competency: judging a setup by
   function, and recognizing when a setup that differs from a
   practitioner's own habits is still doing its job correctly.

`m7cp1` (prep-sequence planning reasoning) follows the signature
interaction; `m7cp2` (live mid-service positioning/comfort adjustment)
closes the module. This is a diagnostic, before-the-fact payoff — "spot
what's wrong before it becomes a problem" — distinct from Module 6's
after-the-fact interpretive payoff.

## Presenter emphasis

Calm, precise, and practitioner-first — the tone of someone who has
built and rebuilt this system many times, not someone selling a room
setup. The student should come away understanding that professional
judgment (does this setup support positioning, reach, service flow, and
comfort?) matters more than copying any one practitioner's exact room.
Keep the safety content — the "why this matters" note and the
stop/adjust/communicate/resume sequence — factual, brief, and
non-alarmist, consistent with the governing principle that safety is a
guardrail, not the module's dominant tone. The presenter should not
sound cautious about the equipment/positioning content generally; the
safety note is one clear, controlled beat within a mostly confident,
functional module, not the emotional center of the video.
