## The script

### M7-01 — Module Briefing (spoken)
**VOICE:** Teaching.

> Welcome to Module 7: Equipment and Room Setup. Before the service starts, your setup is already speaking.
>
> By the time your hands touch the client, they've already formed an impression — not consciously, but they feel whether you're prepared, whether the space is controlled, whether the service will be smooth or interrupted. Your setup is not separate from the service. It is part of it.
>
> In this module, you'll set up a treatment bed that works for the service, not against it; know your tools and supplies well enough to prep without thinking twice; build a repeatable station-prep sequence you can trust under pressure; position a client for comfort, access, and safety, every time; and practice the judgment calls that come up in real station setup.
>
> [warmly] Pay attention to this: a client can't see your prep, but they can feel it — a controlled setup is part of the service itself.

### M7-02 — 7.1 The treatment bed
**SOURCE:** all 7 bed-evaluation categories, full text, no compression. **VOICE:** Teaching for the intro; Reference for the 7 categories; first-person for the Cadence note.

> Section 7.1 — The treatment bed. Your most important equipment decision.
>
> On screen, a fully configured head spa bed, ready for service.
>
> The head spa bed is the foundation of everything. Comfort, positioning, water flow, massage access, client perception — it all builds from this. You can perform a service without the right bed. But you cannot create a premium experience without one.
>
> A halo-equipped wet bed is the foundation of a true head spa service. The halo allows water immersion during massage, which changes the client's physical and psychological state in a way that dry work cannot replicate. Evaluate any bed you're considering against what it actually needs to accomplish — not against how it looks in a photo.
>
> What most people get wrong: they choose based on how it looks, not how it performs. A bed with restrictive armrests may photograph beautifully — but larger clients feel confined, taller clients feel compressed, and movement becomes limited. Once comfort breaks, the entire service is affected. Nothing will compensate for it.
>
> Evaluate the bed against what it needs to do — not how it looks. Run any bed you're offered or can afford against these seven categories.
>
> One: basin and head support. The headrest curve should actually support the occipital bone in a relaxed, non-extended position — this connects directly to correct client positioning in Section 7.4.
>
> Two: client entry, exit, and stability. A client should be able to get on and off the bed safely, and the bed shouldn't shift or wobble under normal movement.
>
> Three: practitioner reach and working height. The bed's height and access affect your own posture and reach across an entire shift — not just a single service.
>
> Four: water management. How water is contained and drained affects both service flow and how easily the station can be sanitized between clients.
>
> Five: cleaning and sanitation compatibility. Surfaces and seams need to be things you can actually disinfect between clients — not just wipe at a glance.
>
> Six: space requirements — a preference, not a universal standard. How much room a bed needs depends on your specific space and business. This is a practitioner-preference and business-context decision, not a documented requirement.
>
> And seven: armrest configuration — a preference, not a requirement. Armrests that tuck in larger or taller clients are a commonly reported comfort complaint among experienced practitioners — a preference many share, not a documented safety or ergonomic requirement. The deciding factor is always how a given bed performs for your actual clientele.
>
> There is no single required bed model — evaluating any bed against these categories, instead of how it photographs, is what makes the decision transferable to whatever you're actually offered or can afford.
>
> Here's a note from me: a common early mistake is prioritizing what looks impressive in photos over what actually feels comfortable to lie in for an hour. Choose the bed your clients will want to come back to — not the one that photographs well.

### M7-03 — 7.2 Tools and supplies
**SOURCE:** all 4 tool categories, every essential/upgrade item named individually (28 items), plus all 3 cart-reach zones. **VOICE:** Teaching for the intro; Reference for the 4 categories and 3 reach zones.

> Section 7.2 — Tools and supplies. Essentials first. Upgrades later.
>
> Most people overvalue tools. They assume more tools means a better service. That's incorrect. Your hands create the experience. Tools support, enhance, and assist — but they should never replace presence. Overuse of tools leads to a mechanical-feeling service, loss of connection, and inconsistent pressure. Clients may not say it. But they feel it.
>
> Start with everything on the essentials list. Nothing on the upgrades list is necessary to deliver a premium service — add from the upgrade tier when your volume justifies it.
>
> On screen, four categories: linens and comfort, service tools, sanitation supplies, and the ambient experience. Here's what's essential and what's an upgrade in each.
>
> Linens and comfort. Essential: fresh bed sheets, a minimum of two sets per station; towels — hand and large, a minimum of six each; a towel warmer; spa wraps and robes; a knee bolster; and an eye mask. Upgrade: a weighted blanket, and a heated jade eye mask.
>
> Service tools. Essential: a rolling cart, since stations should be mobile; mixing bowls — three to four small dishes for product portions; applicator brushes; a wide-tooth comb and detangling brush; sectioning clips; and a blow dryer. Upgrade: a scalp microscope or dermatoscope, a dry brushing tool, and a steamer hood attachment.
>
> Sanitation supplies. Essential: Barbicide concentrate and a properly sized container; EPA-registered disinfecting wipes; nitrile gloves; separate bins for clean tools versus dirty tools — never mix them; and manufacturer-specific halo line cleaner.
>
> Ambient experience. Essential: a water-resistant Bluetooth speaker; a curated, non-lyric playlist with a consistent tone; and an essential oil diffuser. Upgrade: dimmable overhead lighting, LED bias lighting or backlit panels, and an ambient sound machine.
>
> Arranging your cart: where something lives on your cart matters as much as whether you own it. Arrange by how often you reach for it during a service — not by the four categories we just covered. Those categories are for deciding what to buy; this is for how fast you can work once the client is in the room.
>
> The within-reach zone: shoulder-to-waist height, no step required — the three product dishes, the applicator brush, and anything you touch during nearly every step of the service. This is what makes the pressure test later in this module — can I reach everything without stepping away — actually achievable.
>
> The one-step zone: items used once or twice per service — your comb or detangling brush, sectioning clips, blow dryer, spa wrap or robe.
>
> And the reserve zone, off the working surface entirely: backup linens, upgrade-tier tools not in use for this service, and sanitation concentrate stock. Keeping these off the working surface reduces clutter and keeps your clean-and-dirty separation clean and visible.
>
> This is also where the clean-and-dirty bin separation from your sanitation supplies earns its place — it only does its job if it's maintained during the live service, not just set up before the client arrives.

### M7-04 — 7.3 Station prep sequence
**SOURCE:** all 10 prep steps, in order, full detail. **VOICE:** Teaching for the intro; Reference for the sequence.

> Section 7.3 — Station prep sequence. Run this before every single client.
>
> On screen, a station set up in reach order — the items used most, kept closest.
>
> Inconsistency in setup is invisible to you and obvious to your client. A client who experienced a perfectly set room last visit notices when the towel warmer is cold or the cart is in the wrong place — even if they can't articulate why the experience felt off. Build a prep sequence and run it the same way every time.
>
> The order below isn't arbitrary — it follows a build sequence: sanitation and structural setup first, then product and tool staging, then comfort, then ambiance, so nothing has to be assembled while the room is being sanitized or the client is already in it.
>
> One: halo flush. A Barbicide rinse, then plain water until clear.
>
> Two: product dishes. Three bowls — shampoo, mask, exfoliant — with the applicator brush alongside.
>
> Three: towel warmer loaded. Confirm it's warm to the touch before the client arrives.
>
> Four: fresh bed linens. Sheet, headrest cover, and a clean pillow if used.
>
> Five: bed warmer on, set to an appropriate temperature.
>
> Six: room temperature, set lower than normal, since the client will be wet.
>
> Seven: spa wrap, robe, and slipper socks, folded and ready at the station entry.
>
> Eight: a towel wrapped around the halo nozzle hose. It quiets it, prevents spray, and maintains atmosphere.
>
> Nine: massage oil and lotion, ready on the cart and easily accessible.
>
> And ten: ambient — music, lighting, diffuser — running before the client enters the room.

### M7-05 — 7.4 Client positioning
**SOURCE:** both photo-pair figcaptions, all 3 positioning checks, full safety/watch-for/what-to-do guidance, the Cadence note, and the pressure test. **VOICE:** Teaching for the intro; Reference for the 3 checks and the safety guidance; first-person for the Cadence note.

> Section 7.4 — Client positioning. Three things to confirm before the water runs.
>
> On screen, two side-by-side photos. The first: correct — occipital supported, neck relaxed, shoulders clear of the edge. The second: incorrect — chin lifted, neck extended over the basin edge. That's the exact position to avoid.
>
> Positioning errors are hard to correct mid-service without breaking the experience. Get these right before you start — it takes thirty seconds and prevents a disruption you cannot undo.
>
> One: halo alignment. The client centered under the halo so water hits evenly across the scalp. Adjust the client's position — not the halo.
>
> Two: shoulder position. The top of the shoulders sits one to two inches off the edge of the bed. This gives you clean access to the neck without the client sliding.
>
> Three: occipital support. The occipital bone rests in the deepest curve of the headrest, with the neck naturally relaxed — not extended, not flexed. If the chin is lifting, reposition.
>
> Why this matters: a relaxed, neutral neck position isn't just about comfort — sustained hyperextension against a hard basin edge is a documented, if rare, safety concern. Keeping the neck supported and unextended, as the three checks above describe, is the setup detail most directly within a practitioner's control to reduce that risk — not a guarantee against it.
>
> [firmly] Watch for: the client reports neck strain, dizziness, or discomfort at any point, or the chin is visibly lifting. Some discomfort signals are only about comfort; if a client ever reports dizziness, visual changes, or slurred speech, stop immediately and treat it as a medical concern, not a positioning fix.
>
> What to do: stop the service, adjust the positioning or temperature issue, communicate what you're doing and check in with the client, then resume only once she confirms she's comfortable. Never adjust silently or push through to a natural stopping point.
>
> [slowly] Here's a note from me: cover the client first, then slide the knee bolster into place. Weighted blanket across the chest if using. Eye mask on. Then begin. Doing it out of sequence makes the client feel arranged rather than cared for.
>
> Pressure test your setup: ask yourself — if something unexpected happens, can I adjust without breaking flow? Can I reach everything without stepping away? Does this still work when I'm not thinking perfectly? If not, your setup is not ready.

### M7-06 — Signature interaction: Find the setup mistakes
**SOURCE:** all 4 cards' prompts and reveals, single reveal per card (no per-choice branch — confirmed from `m7CardAnswer()`). **VOICE:** Reference for all 4.

> Now for a signature interaction: find the setup mistakes. Not every difference is a mistake. Look at each setup and decide whether something actually needs to change — or whether it's simply a different, functional way of working.
>
> Setup one: the cart is positioned on the practitioner's left. Does anything here actually need to change? Acceptable variation. Different does not automatically mean wrong — the cart can sit on either side as long as active-use items remain comfortably reachable and the setup supports efficient service flow. Reachability matters. Left versus right does not.
>
> Setup two: the items used throughout the service are positioned well outside the immediate working area. Does this setup need correction? Yes — needs correction. Frequently used items belong within comfortable working reach. Repeatedly stepping away for active-use products interrupts service flow and signals that the station needs reorganizing. Frequency of use should influence placement.
>
> Setup three: this treatment bed does not have optional armrests. Is that itself a setup problem? Not necessarily — acceptable variation. Equipment should be judged by function, support, and service usability — not by whether it includes every optional feature. Armrests may be a practitioner preference rather than a universal requirement. Equipment preference is not the same as a functional requirement.
>
> And setup four: look at the client's head, neck, and shoulder position. Does this setup need correction? Yes — needs correction. The client is positioned too far from the head end, leaving the occipital area poorly supported and the neck extended. Reposition the client before continuing. What to remember: professional setup is judged by function — support, reach, comfort, and service flow — not by whether every treatment room looks identical.
