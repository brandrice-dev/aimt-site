## The script

### M5-01 — Module Briefing (spoken)
**VOICE:** Teaching.

> Welcome to Module 5. Module 4 taught you how to gather and describe evidence. Now you're going to turn supported observations, what the client reports, and regional differences into an actual cosmetic service plan. The goal isn't to assign one permanent scalp type — it's to make each step earn its place.
>
> Here's what's ahead: you'll turn evidence and observation into an actual service plan, learn what you can realistically change in a single appointment, set priority order when more than one concern is present, adapt one protocol to more than one client in real time, and recognize the errors that make a protocol less intelligent, not more.
>
> [warmly] Pay attention to this: a scalp pattern is a starting point, not a permanent label. The plan should adapt as the evidence does.

### M5-02 — 5.1 From evidence to action
**VOICE:** Teaching, Reference for the 4-question list.

> Section 5.1 — From evidence to action. The scalp does not need a label. The service needs a reason.
>
> A useful assessment changes what you do next. It may change how thoroughly you cleanse, whether you exfoliate, how much heat you use, where you place product, how much pressure you apply, or whether you pause the service entirely.
>
> The weak approach is to notice one feature, assign one type, and run the same protocol across the entire scalp. The stronger approach is to combine what is visible with what the client reports, what happened recently, and how each region responds.
>
> One client may show diffuse shine at the crown, fine loose scale near the hairline, and no discomfort anywhere. Another may show very little visible buildup but report burning after a recent product change. Those clients do not need the same service — and neither one can be reduced to one word.
>
> Ask four questions before you choose a step. One: what is visibly present? Two: what does the client report? Three: what is the safest service limit? And four: what should change in this region?
>
> [firmly] Limit first. Priority second. Region by region.

### M5-03 — 5.2 What you can actually change
**VOICE:** Reference for all 5 levers.

> Section 5.2 — What you can actually change. Most protocol changes come from five levers. Skilled customization is usually not a completely different service — it is a deliberate change in intensity, placement, duration, or omission.
>
> One, cleansing: decide how thorough the cleanse should be, whether one region needs more attention than another, and whether repeating a cleanse is appropriate. More lather is not the goal. A clean, comfortable finish is.
>
> Two, exfoliation: decide whether exfoliation belongs at all. If it does, choose the region, method, intensity, and contact time carefully. Visible scale is not automatic permission to exfoliate.
>
> Three, water and steam: adjust temperature, duration, and distance. Heat can increase comfort for some clients and increase discomfort for others. Steam is optional — not proof that the service is more advanced.
>
> Four, pressure and tempo: adjust mechanical stimulation to the client's comfort and the current region. Pressure is not a corrective tool. Firm massage should never be used to overpower tenderness, burning, or a reactive presentation.
>
> And five, product placement and finish: choose where product belongs, how much is needed, and whether the finish should be lightweight, conditioning, or minimal. Whole-scalp application is not automatically more thorough.
>
> [slowly] Product comes after the decision. Do not choose the protocol because you want to use a particular product. Choose the service direction first, then select a compatible product category within your license, training, and manufacturer instructions.

### M5-04 — 5.3 Priority order + Cadence note
**VOICE:** Reference for all 5 priority items; first-person for the Cadence note.

> Section 5.3 — Priority order. The safest limit outranks the client's request. Clients may ask for the deepest cleanse, the strongest exfoliation, maximum steam, or firm pressure. Their preference matters — but it does not override what the assessment and consultation tell you is appropriate today.
>
> One, safety limit: is there any reason to avoid the area, pause, or refer? Broken, bleeding, weeping, oozing, draining, pustular, severely painful, rapidly worsening, or otherwise concerning findings do not need a stronger cosmetic protocol.
>
> Two, client comfort and reactivity: ask about stinging, burning, tenderness, itching, heat, tightness, recent chemical services, scratching, friction, or a recent product reaction.
>
> Three, surface tolerance: consider whether cleansing, exfoliation, heat, or friction is likely to make the current presentation less comfortable.
>
> Four, visible cosmetic need: decide what oil, residue, loose scale, or regional difference can be addressed conservatively within the service.
>
> And five, client preference and maintenance goal: once the limits are clear, shape the experience around the client's comfort, maintenance needs, and desired level of relaxation.
>
> [warmly] Here's a note from me before we move on. The strongest service is not the one with the most steps. It is the one in which every step has a reason and nothing is added simply to prove value.

### M5-05 — 5.4 Patterns, not permanent types
**SOURCE:** all 5 patterns, all 4 fields each, no compression. **VOICE:** Reference throughout.

> Section 5.4 — Patterns, not permanent types. Read the dominant need without forcing the whole scalp into it.
>
> Pattern A, baseline or maintenance presentation. What may be present: no dominant cosmetic concern, no meaningful regional difference, and no reported discomfort — the client may simply want relaxation, maintenance, and a consistent routine. Context still needed: wash timing, recent product use, service goal, allergies, sensitivities, and any recent chemical service. Responsible direction: preserve. Use a gentle, non-disruptive cleanse, avoid unnecessary exfoliation, and let the service focus on comfort and maintenance. Do not conclude that the scalp has one universal "healthy" color, or that every corrective step should be added because the client paid for a premium service.
>
> Pattern B, oil-dominant or residue-present presentation. What may be present: diffuse shine, slick roots, visible coating, adherent surface material, or heavier material in selected regions. Context still needed: wash timing, dry shampoo, root products, exercise, sweat, work environment, headwear, recent styling products, and whether the material changes after cleansing. Responsible direction: cleanse thoroughly but proportionately. Use targeted product placement, repeat cleansing only when appropriate, consider compatible localized exfoliation when the surface is intact and comfortable, rinse fully, and avoid leaving an unnecessarily heavy finish. Do not conclude that the follicles are clogged, that visible material is definitely sebum, that hair growth is being blocked, or that cleansing will trigger guaranteed compensatory oil production.
>
> Pattern C, fine-scale or dry-appearing presentation. What may be present: a matte surface, fine loose scale, little visible surface shine, or a client report of tightness. Context still needed: itching, burning, tenderness, cleansing frequency, shampoo type, chemical services, weather, heat styling, recent product changes, and whether scale is loose or adherent. Responsible direction: lower unnecessary cleansing intensity, use comfortable water temperature, reduce friction, choose a compatible conditioning or hydration-supportive cosmetic product, and exfoliate only when the area is intact, comfortable, and appropriate for the product and service. Do not conclude that the image proves dehydration, a damaged barrier, dandruff, dermatitis, or a need for exfoliation.
>
> Pattern D, mixed regional presentation. What may be present: shine or residue in one region and fine scale, matte appearance, or greater sensitivity in another. Context still needed: regional product placement, wash habits, dry shampoo use, chemical-service history, styling patterns, friction, and symptoms by area. Responsible direction: map the regions. Cleanse, exfoliate, condition, or reduce stimulation only where each decision is earned — one client may receive more thorough cleansing at the crown, a gentler approach at the hairline, and a maintenance approach elsewhere. Do not conclude that one dominant feature should control the protocol across the entire scalp.
>
> Pattern E, reactive presentation or sensitivity reported. What may be present: a visible difference from surrounding baseline combined with client-reported stinging, burning, tenderness, itching, heat, tightness, or a recent reaction. Context still needed: recent chemical service, product change, heat exposure, scratching, friction, known allergies, symptom severity, broken skin, drainage, pustules, crusting, or rapid worsening. Responsible direction: reduce stimulation. Lower heat, use cooler or comfortably lukewarm water, avoid aggressive exfoliation, avoid firm massage over the area, and use fewer familiar compatible products — pause or refer when the presentation falls outside cosmetic service limits. Do not conclude that visible color alone proves sensitivity, inflammation, infection, or the medical cause.
>
> [slowly] On screen, you'll see a real case study — the same client's crown and hairline, side by side. The crown shows greater visible shine and surface coating, which may support more targeted cleansing there. The hairline and temporal area show a more matte appearance with fine visible scale, which may call for a gentler service direction instead. This is illustrative only — appearance alone does not establish cause or diagnosis.

### M5-06 — Signature interaction: What changes first?
**SOURCE:** all 4 scenarios, all 3 options and their exact feedback text — see audit's Judgment call note. **VOICE:** Reference throughout.

> Signature interaction: what changes first? Choose the first responsible service direction. You are not diagnosing the scalp — you are deciding what the current evidence allows you to do next. There are four scenarios on screen, each with three possible directions. I'll walk through all three choices for each one.
>
> Scenario one. The five-point assessment shows no dominant concern or meaningful regional difference. The client reports no discomfort and wants a relaxation-focused service.
> First option, add a strong exfoliation so the service feels complete — not quite: no finding currently earns a stronger corrective step, and adding exfoliation simply to make the service feel complete would override the evidence rather than respond to it. Second option, preserve the current presentation and customize for relaxation — correct: a stable presentation does not need to be turned into a corrective project, and gentle cleansing, comfort, and maintenance are enough. Third option, use a clarifying protocol to prevent future buildup — not quite: the current assessment does not show a need for corrective cleansing, so preserve the stable presentation instead of treating a future problem that has not been observed.
>
> Scenario two. The crown shows diffuse shine and visible surface residue. The sides and hairline look close to the client's baseline. The client reports no burning, tenderness, or recent reaction and says they used dry shampoo this morning.
> First option, use targeted cleansing at the crown and keep the remaining regions gentle — correct: the recent dry-shampoo history and regional difference support targeted cleansing, and the image does not prove clogged follicles or justify whole-scalp overcorrection. Second option, exfoliate the entire scalp aggressively — not quite: the finding is regional, and the dry-shampoo history gives useful context, so whole-scalp aggressive exfoliation would overcorrect areas that do not show the same need. Third option, tell the client the crown follicles are clogged — not quite: visible shine and residue do not prove that follicles are clogged, so stay with what you can observe and choose a cosmetic service adjustment from that evidence.
>
> Scenario three. The hairline shows fine loose scale. The client reports burning after starting a new product three days ago.
> First option, exfoliate the scale first so hydration can penetrate — not quite: the client's burning changes the priority, and visible scale is not automatic permission to exfoliate, especially when the area is currently reactive or uncomfortable. Second option, reduce stimulation, avoid aggressive exfoliation, and reassess whether the service should continue — correct: the client's burning changes the priority, and comfort and safety come before scale removal. Third option, increase steam to soften the area — not quite: more heat is not the responsible first move when the client is reporting burning — reduce stimulation and reassess before adding intensity.
>
> Scenario four. One assessment area is broken, moist, and visibly draining.
> First option, avoid only that small spot and complete the rest of the service — not quite: broken, draining skin changes the service limit, and this is not a situation where you simply work around the area and continue as usual. Second option, clean the area thoroughly before proceeding — not quite: a cosmetic head spa service should not be used to clean or treat broken, draining skin — stop contact, pause the service, and use the appropriate referral pathway. Third option, stop contact with the area, pause the service, and refer appropriately — correct: a cosmetic protocol is not the next step when the skin is broken and draining.
>
> [firmly] Across all four, the same logic holds: preserve what does not need correcting, target the specific evidence you actually have, reduce stimulation when there is reported discomfort, and stop contact entirely — pause and refer — the moment skin integrity is in question.

### M5-07 — 5.5 One appointment, more than one approach
**VOICE:** Teaching for the intro; Reference for the 5-category framework and the practitioner note.

> Section 5.5 — One appointment, more than one approach. The protocol can change without the service feeling fragmented. Regional customization should feel intentional, not improvised — use the same service structure, then adjust the levers by area.
>
> You might cleanse the crown more thoroughly, skip exfoliation near a reactive hairline, lower pressure at the temples, and use a lighter finish at the roots. The client still experiences one cohesive service. The practitioner simply stops pretending that every square inch has the same need.
>
> Preserve: keep the steps and regions that are already appropriate — do not remove a comfortable, effective part of the service just because another area needs modification. Modify: change intensity, placement, duration, temperature, pressure, or product amount. Avoid: omit a step that is unnecessary, incompatible, or likely to increase discomfort. Pause: stop when new symptoms, visible changes, contamination, or client discomfort make continuing inappropriate. And refer: recommend medical evaluation when the presentation is outside cosmetic assessment or service limits.
>
> [slowly] A practitioner note: document the regional decision in plain language — what you observed, what the client reported, and what you changed. Do not document a diagnosis you are not qualified to make.
>
> On screen, you'll see a practitioner sectioning the crown and applying cleansing product specifically at the roots. Regional customization may change product placement and cleansing intensity without changing the entire service.

### M5-10 — 5.6 Choose a function, not a fantasy
**VOICE:** Teaching for the intro; Reference for the 5 product-function categories and the boundary note.

> Section 5.6 — Choose a function, not a fantasy. The right product is the one that supports the decision you already made. Product knowledge matters, but product enthusiasm can distort judgment — a practitioner who starts with the bottle often finds a reason to use it. Start with the service need instead, and choose a product category based on what the current region can tolerate and what you are trying to accomplish cosmetically.
>
> Gentle routine cleansing — appropriate when the goal is maintenance, comfort, or a non-disruptive reset. Targeted or more thorough cosmetic cleansing — appropriate when visible oil or residue and client history support a stronger cleanse in selected regions. Optional compatible exfoliation — appropriate only when the skin is intact, the client is comfortable, the method is within scope, and the product directions support its use. Conditioning or hydration support — appropriate when the goal is comfort, slip, softness, or a less stripping finish; do not describe the product as treating a medical barrier disorder. And a minimal-product service — appropriate when the client reports recent reactivity, when product history is unclear, or when adding more variables would make the response harder to interpret.
>
> [firmly] A boundary note: specific ingredients, medicated claims, and condition-directed products may carry license, labeling, contraindication, or manufacturer requirements. Follow the product — not a social-media protocol.

### M5-11 — 5.7 Intensity is part of the protocol
**VOICE:** Reference for all 4 factors.

> Section 5.7 — Intensity is part of the protocol. More heat, more pressure, and more time do not equal more expertise.
>
> Steam: it may support comfort or help soften some surface product and residue, but it is optional. Reduce or omit it when the client reports heat sensitivity, burning, tenderness, or a recent reaction — and do not say steam opens pores or detoxifies the scalp.
>
> Water temperature: comfortably lukewarm is a useful default. Use cooler water when heat increases discomfort, and avoid very hot water on a reactive or uncomfortable area.
>
> Pressure: it should remain controlled and responsive to client feedback. Do not use firm massage as a corrective response to oil, scale, or residue.
>
> And time: longer contact is not automatically better. Follow manufacturer directions and use the shortest effective step that supports the service goal.
>
> [slowly] The protocol is not the number of steps. It is the quality of the decisions between them.
>
> On screen, you'll see a practitioner using light fingertip contact and minimal product at the client's hairline. Lower pressure, less product, and reduced stimulation can be deliberate protocol decisions.

### M5-12 — 5.8 Client communication + Cadence note
**SOURCE:** all 3 scripts, verbatim. **VOICE:** Teaching for the intro; Reference for the 3 quoted scripts; first-person for the Cadence note.

> Section 5.8 — Client communication. Redirect the method without dismissing the goal. Clients often ask for intensity because they associate intensity with results. Your job is not to argue with the goal — it's to explain why a different method is the better fit today.
>
> On screen, a practitioner explains a personalized regional scalp-service plan to a seated client — what was observed, what the client reported, and why the service plan changed.
>
> Here's the mixed-regional script: "Your scalp is not presenting the same way in every area today. I'm going to cleanse the crown more thoroughly, keep the hairline gentler, and adjust the product placement so we do not overcorrect the areas that do not need it."
>
> The lower-intensity script: "You asked for the deepest version of the service. Based on what you're feeling and what I'm observing, the stronger exfoliation, maximum steam, and firm pressure are not the best fit today. I can still give you a complete service, but I'm going to lower the heat, reduce stimulation, and focus on a gentle cleanse and comfort."
>
> And the pause-and-refer script: "I'm seeing an area that is not appropriate for cosmetic treatment today. I do not want to aggravate it or guess at the cause, so I recommend having it evaluated before we continue with scalp services."
>
> [warmly] One thing I'd add before we move on: client satisfaction is not created by saying yes to every requested step. It is created by giving the client a clear reason, a thoughtful alternative when one is appropriate, and a service plan you can defend.

### M5-13 — 5.9 Errors that make protocols less intelligent
**SOURCE:** all 8 error patterns with their fixes. **VOICE:** Reference throughout.

> Section 5.9 — Errors that make protocols less intelligent. The most common mistake is treating the label instead of the client.
>
> Label first — the mistake is choosing "oily," "dry," or "sensitive" before gathering regional evidence and client history. The better move: gather the regional evidence and client context first, then choose the service direction each area actually supports.
>
> Product first — the mistake is building the explanation around the product you want to use instead of the need you are trying to address. The better move: define the service need first, then choose a compatible product category that supports that decision.
>
> Whole scalp, one intensity — the mistake is applying the strongest regional need across every assessment area. The better move: map the regions and adjust cleansing, exfoliation, heat, pressure, and product placement only where the evidence supports it.
>
> Exfoliating every flake — the mistake is treating visible scale as proof that exfoliation is required. The better move: check comfort, skin integrity, client history, and product suitability before deciding whether exfoliation belongs at all.
>
> Strongest means best — the mistake is assuming maximum steam, repeated cleansing, firm pressure, or more product creates a more valuable service. The better move: use the level of cleansing, heat, pressure, time, and product placement the current service goal actually requires — not the maximum available.
>
> Image becomes diagnosis — the mistake is turning shine, scale, residue, or color change into a medical conclusion. The better move: describe what is visible, ask for the missing context, and keep the conclusion inside cosmetic observation.
>
> Client request becomes permission — the mistake is continuing an inappropriate step because the client asked for it. The better move: explain the safer modification, or pause the service when the requested step exceeds the current service limit.
>
> And improvement becomes cure — the mistake is describing a cleaner appearance or more comfortable service as proof that a condition was treated. The better move: describe the cosmetic change you can observe or the comfort the client reports, without claiming that a condition was treated or cured.
>
> [firmly] A strong protocol is explainable. You should be able to name what you observed, what context changed your interpretation, what you modified, and what you intentionally left out.
