## The script

### M9-01 — Module Briefing (spoken)
**VOICE:** Teaching.

> Welcome to Module 9. The treatment is done — what happens next, how you close the appointment and how you price what you do, is its own skill. This module is a business decision lab: less about performing, more about deciding, from real numbers and calm communication.
>
> You'll close the appointment as intentionally as you opened it, know your real costs and the difference between margin and markup, price a service using market context rather than copying a competitor, design a menu and enhancements that actually earn their place, and respond calmly when a client says the price felt high.
>
> [warmly] Pay attention to this: pricing conversations go wrong far more often from how they're delivered than from the number itself.

### M9-02 — 9.1 From treatment close to checkout + Close Without Pressure
**SOURCE:** 9.1's full checkout shape and platform-neutral note + the 6-option interaction (framing + correct pattern, wrong-answer categories, choice left to student). **VOICE:** Teaching, Reference for the checkout shape and platform-neutral note.

> Section 9.1 — From treatment close to checkout. The moment isn't over when the treatment is.
>
> Module 8 ends with one line: "Today I focused a little more on this area — how are you feeling?" That's a treatment-closing observation, not a business conversation. What happens after it is this module's subject. It's a shape, not a script: reorient, briefly recap, answer or recommend where relevant, invite future options without pressure, complete checkout — not a rigid order, just a natural sequence.
>
> Give the client a moment to reorient before you speak further — a quiet, relaxation-first service shouldn't end with an abrupt shift into a sales pitch. Recap only what you genuinely did; don't diagnose, don't exaggerate. Answer questions clearly without unloading every recommendation at once — and the flow has to work cleanly for a client who says no to everything. Declining should never create awkwardness.
>
> [slowly] Rebooking is an available next step, not proof you successfully "closed." Help the client choose what fits their goals and schedule — don't manufacture a future booking. And checkout itself stays platform-neutral: confirm what they're paying for, communicate the total clearly, process payment through your own normal system, provide a receipt. Gratuity, if your business accepts it, stays neutral in presentation — never mandatory, never a fixed percentage.
>
> Close without pressure: a client has just finished an Extended service. She's sitting up, taking a slow breath, reorienting. On screen, six possible responses — decide which one best moves toward checkout without breaking the experience you just built.
>
> [firmly] The pattern that actually works: give her a moment, name one specific thing you focused on, ask how she's feeling, then offer checkout and future options without pressure — all at a calm, normal pace. What breaks it: rushing straight into rebooking, reciting the entire service step by step, implying a problem you "fixed," stacking multiple recommendations at once, or creating false urgency about availability. Look at all six and choose the one that actually preserves the experience.

### M9-03 — 9.2 Know the real cost + 9.3 Margin vs. markup
**SOURCE:** all 4 cost inputs + the full markup/margin worked example. **VOICE:** Reference for both.

> Section 9.2 — Know the real cost. Four inputs — not a rigid formula, a way of thinking. Real cost isn't a guess, and it isn't one universal number either.
>
> Direct and variable costs: products, consumables, laundry, disposables — these scale with each service, so track your actual use, not a rough guess. Allocated overhead: rent, utilities, insurance, software, shared supplies — these exist whether you see a client or not, and how you divide them across services is a business decision, not a course requirement. Practitioner time, broader than treatment time: setup, consultation, treatment, transitions, cleanup, checkout, documentation — a sixty-minute service can require meaningfully more than sixty minutes of your capacity. And desired profitability: how much margin you want is a deliberate business decision you can explain, not a default you accept without thinking about it.
>
> Section 9.3 — Margin versus markup. Two words. Two different prices.
>
> Markup is calculated from cost: a 30% markup on a $100 cost is $130. Margin is calculated from selling price: a 30% margin means 30% of the selling price is profit, which means solving price equals cost divided by one minus margin — at a 30% margin, that same $100 cost produces a price of about $143, not $130.
>
> [firmly] Thirty percent markup and thirty percent margin do not produce the same selling price. Know which one you're using.

### M9-04 — 9.4 Price your service + 9.5 Market context
**VOICE:** Teaching.

> Section 9.4 — Price your service. On screen there's a cost-base-to-target-price calculator, using the margin concept you just learned — enter your own product cost, overhead, practitioner time, and target margin, and it solves for the selling price that hits your target. It's a planning tool — a real business may also need to account for taxes, payroll burden, payment processing, insurance, and other costs depending on your structure and jurisdiction. A-I-M-T doesn't prescribe state-specific tax treatment or business structure.
>
> Section 9.5 — Market context, not copycat pricing. Look at the market after you know your own numbers, not instead of them.
>
> A competitor's price may not even cover their own actual costs — copying it risks copying a model that's already losing money. Know your own economics first, then look at your local market; context can legitimately inform expectations, positioning, and demand — it isn't irrelevant, just secondary. [slowly] Final price sits at the intersection of cost structure, service design, capacity, market context, and business positioning — not any one of those alone.

### M9-05 — 9.6 Design your menu + 9.7 Enhancements that earn their place
**SOURCE:** the menu-design principles + all 5 enhancement examples. **VOICE:** Reference for both.

> Section 9.6 — Design your menu. Make it easy to understand. A concise menu is generally easier to navigate — two or three clearly differentiated options can be useful, but the number should follow your actual business, not a universal rule.
>
> Require meaningful differentiation: different menu options should change something real — time, bodywork, processing, enhancements, scope — never an artificial "premium" difference just to create an anchor. And remember: Core and Extended are A-I-M-T's own teaching labels from Module 8, not required client-facing menu names. Your business can package its services however it wants.
>
> Section 9.7 — Enhancements that earn their place. A genuine enhancement has a distinct purpose, a real service difference, a cost effect, and clear positioning — based on the established plan, the client's goals, your observation within scope, and the time you actually have.
>
> Five examples, with real client language for each: a scalp treatment serum — "based on what I'm observing today, I'd suggest adding a treatment serum, it takes about five minutes and supports what we're already doing." Extended massage time — "I noticed some tension through your shoulders today, I can extend your massage by fifteen minutes if you'd like." A deep conditioning upgrade — "your ends could use extra moisture, I can swap in a protein mask and add steam." A blow dry, which you decide in advance to include or offer as an add-on — either is valid, just be consistent. And an aromatherapy enhancement — "we can add a custom aromatherapy blend today, based on your preference."
>
> [firmly] Don't imply "what the scalp needs" — that phrasing crosses into diagnosis. Don't recommend an enhancement just because it's on the menu. And an enhancement recommendation must never override a genuine scalp-presentation safety reason not to add a product — if a presentation calls for restraint, restraint wins.

### M9-07 — Post-pass: 9.8 When a client says the price felt high
**SOURCE:** the full "in the moment" guidance + the near-verbatim model response + the "afterward" review questions. **VOICE:** Reference for the model response, Teaching for framing. `gateType: 'post-pass'`, `resumeAfterPass: true`.

> Section 9.8 — When a client says the price felt high. One comment. Several possible causes.
>
> "That felt expensive" can mean a price-market mismatch, unclear expectations, unclear inclusions, weak differentiation, a genuine affordability issue, one client's individual preference — or sometimes that nothing needs to change at all. Don't diagnose the business problem from one sentence.
>
> In the moment: acknowledge, stay calm, don't argue, don't reach for an immediate discount, don't lecture the client on value, answer a genuine question clearly, preserve their autonomy. Here's a model response, not a required script: "Thank you for telling me. I'm glad you enjoyed the service. Our pricing reflects the time and components included, and I'm happy to explain what was included or help you compare options for a future visit."
>
> Afterward, review the evidence: was the price clear before booking? Were the inclusions clear? Does the price cover your real cost structure? Does the menu communicate meaningful differences? Is this feedback recurring? [slowly] Don't assume the client is wrong. Don't assume the price is wrong.

### M9-08 — 9.9 Why pricing really goes wrong + Checkpoint 2 (`m10cp2`)
**SOURCE:** the underpricing-causes list + the positioning-language reframe pair + checkpoint prompt. **VOICE:** Reference for both.

> Section 9.9 — Why pricing really goes wrong. Confidence is not a financial model.
>
> Underpricing has more causes than fear: incomplete cost data, poor overhead allocation, inaccurate time assumptions, competitor copying, an intentional launch strategy, market constraints, weak differentiation, a lack of pricing knowledge — and yes, sometimes fear. Fear is one cause among several, not the default explanation. Persistent underpricing can contribute to financial strain, unsustainable workload, or inconsistent delivery over time — not guaranteed, but a real pattern worth watching for.
>
> Positioning language matters: "this is our basic option" becomes "this is our core service." "This is more expensive" becomes "this is our extended experience." That's accurate framing, not engineered pressure.
>
> Here's your final checkpoint. At checkout, a client says, "I loved the service, but the price felt high." What would you say in the moment, and what would you review afterward before deciding whether anything about your pricing, menu, or positioning should change?
>
> Take your time, and answer above.
