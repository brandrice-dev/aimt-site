## The script

### M4-01 — Module Briefing (spoken)
**SOURCE:** `.mod-opener`. **VOICE:** Teaching.

> Welcome to Module 4. Magnification doesn't hand you a diagnosis — it gives you a better view. This module teaches a repeatable five-point scan, disciplined observation, and the moment a cosmetic service should be customized, simplified, paused, or referred.
>
> You'll learn that five-point scan, present magnified findings to a client without overstating what you see, apply five observation lenses to real, varied scalp images, and know exactly when to customize, simplify, or pause a service — plus the common mistakes new practitioners make when reading these images.
>
> [warmly] Pay attention to this: two scalps can look nearly identical and still call for two different decisions. The image is evidence — not the whole story.

### M4-02 — 4.1 The role of magnification + 4.2 Presenting the assessment
**SOURCE:** Sections 4.1–4.2, both quoted scripts verbatim. **VOICE:** Teaching for framing, Reference for both quoted scripts.

> Section 4.1 — The role of magnification. A closer view is not a final answer.
>
> A scalp camera can reveal details easy to miss with the unaided eye — surface shine, scale, residue, material around follicular openings, hair-shaft variation, differences from one region to another. That doesn't mean the device knows what caused what you see. Medical trichoscopy is a clinical diagnostic technique, interpreted alongside history, examination, and other medical information. In a head spa, scalp microscopy is a cosmetic observation and education tool. Its value is precision, not certainty — it helps you collect better evidence before choosing a cosmetic service direction.
>
> [slowly] A weak assessment names the scalp too quickly. A strong assessment collects the view, compares regions, asks for context, and lets the service decision remain no more certain than the evidence.
>
> Section 4.2 — Presenting the assessment. Make the lens feel informative, not invasive.
>
> Tell the client what the camera is for before it touches the scalp. Here's the live-view script: "Before we begin, I'm going to look at several areas of your scalp under magnification. I'll use what is visible—along with what you tell me—to customize the cosmetic service. The camera helps us see detail, but it does not diagnose a scalp condition."
>
> And if you're capturing images: "I can use the camera for a live view without saving anything. I can also capture comparison images for your service record. Would you like me to save them, and may I explain how they will be stored and used?"
>
> Permission to view is not permission to save. Permission to save for the client record is not permission to post, teach from, transmit to another service, or use in marketing.
>
> Show the image without making the client feel dirty, damaged, or alarmed. Describe the finding. Explain the cosmetic decision. State the limit when the cause can't be determined.

### M4-03 — 4.3 Image integrity
**SOURCE:** Section 4.3, all 6 steps + sanitation note. **VOICE:** Reference for all 6 steps in order, Teaching for framing.

> Section 4.3 — Image integrity. A useful image begins before the lens touches the scalp. Magnification can exaggerate a poor setup — standardize the conditions before you interpret the screen.
>
> One: start before treatment — assess before water, exfoliants, oils, masks, steam, or treatment products alter the surface. Two: ask what's already on the scalp — wash timing, dry shampoo, root sprays, oils, fibers, medicated products, chemical services, recent scratching or irritation. Three: part cleanly, without scraping or repeatedly disturbing the same area. Four: touch lightly — excess pressure can blanch or redden the skin and create the finding you think you discovered. Five: hold the view long enough to actually inspect it; a constant sweep produces motion, blur, and shallow interpretation. And six: make comparisons honest — match region, magnification, light, pressure, orientation, and device settings when comparing two images.
>
> [firmly] Clean and disinfect reusable contact surfaces before and after each client, per the manufacturer's instructions and applicable rules. Never place the device on broken, bleeding, weeping, oozing, or draining skin. If the setup changes, the image changes — document the difference before you call it improvement.

### M4-04 — 4.4 Five-point scan + 4.5 Five observation lenses + Observation discipline
**SOURCE:** Section 4.4 (5 stations), 4.5 (5 lenses), the classification exercise (framing, choice left to student). **VOICE:** Reference for both 5-item lists, Teaching for framing.

> Section 4.4 — The five-point scalp scan. The scalp isn't uniform — AIMT's baseline scan uses the same five assessment points, in the same order, every time, so one dramatic image never gets to represent the entire head. The memory line: front, top, crown, side, back.
>
> On screen there's a reference photo for each of the five stations — frontal hairline, top parting, crown and vertex, temporal area, and occipital, the back of the head. The frontal hairline is where styling products, tension, and scale are often easiest to notice. The top parting gives you a consistent central view of follicular openings and hair distribution. The crown gets compared against the parting and hairline rather than assumed to match. The temporal area lets you compare a lateral region against the center — check the opposite side if asymmetry or a localized concern comes up. And the occipital, the hidden back of the head, may look different from the areas the client sees and styles most often — position the hair securely so the lens stays steady.
>
> A five-point scan creates the baseline. The client's concern tells you where to add targeted views.
>
> Section 4.5 — The five observation lenses. Don't label the scalp — read the evidence. Use the same five lenses at every assessment area.
>
> Scalp surface: color relative to the client's own baseline, sheen, loose or adherent scale, visible coating, whether the skin is intact. Document like this — "diffuse surface shine at the crown with small areas of adherent yellow-white material."
>
> Follicular openings: whether they're visible, and whether material is present at or near them. Document like this — "several openings show visible material around the emerging shafts." Never write "the follicles are clogged."
>
> Perifollicular area: color change, scale, or visible material immediately surrounding a follicle.
>
> Hair shafts: visible breakage, coating, residue, variation in shaft appearance, how many shafts are in the field — but one image can't establish the cause of breakage or thinning.
>
> And distribution: whether the finding is localized, diffuse, symmetric, or different from another assessment area. Distribution is often more useful than the most dramatic close-up — one image is a detail, the scan is the pattern.
>
> Now — observation discipline. On screen, you'll classify five real statements as a supported observation, a working question, or an unsupported conclusion. Things like "the crown shows diffuse shine and visible yellow-white material" versus "this is seborrheic dermatitis." [firmly] Strong assessment language is precise about what's visible, and disciplined about what remains unknown.

### M4-06 — Post-pass: 4.6 Appearance examples + Similar image, different story
**SOURCE:** Section 4.6, all 5 illustrative cards (compressed) + the photo-pair comparison + its 5-question list. **VOICE:** Reference for all 5 categories and the 5-question list. `gateType: 'post-pass'`, `resumeAfterPass: true`.

> Section 4.6 — Appearance examples. These build visual vocabulary — illustrative magnified examples, not authenticated clinical captures, and not proof of a diagnosis. There's a reference photo on screen for each one. Actual scalp appearance varies by client, skin tone, lighting, magnification, device, and product history.
>
> Five categories, each with the same discipline: what's visible, what context you'd still need, how it might shift your service, and — just as important — what it does not prove.
>
> A baseline-appearing view: relatively even surface, visible follicular openings, minimal scale. It doesn't prove the scalp is universally healthy or free of anything outside the camera's view — the move here is to avoid unnecessary correction and follow the client's own goals.
>
> An oil-dominant appearance: diffuse shine and sebum-like material around follicular openings. It doesn't prove overproduction, poor hygiene, dandruff, or infection — after checking context like wash timing and product use, it may support a more thorough but still conservative cleansing direction.
>
> A fine-scale, dry-appearing surface: matte, with fine loose scale. It doesn't prove dehydration, barrier damage, or dermatitis — it may support a gentler, simplified service, avoiding aggressive cleansing or exfoliation.
>
> A visible color-change or reactive-appearing area: color variation, possibly with scale. It doesn't prove sensitivity, inflammation, allergy, or infection — reduce pressure, heat, friction, and product load, and avoid the area entirely if the client reports discomfort.
>
> And surface residue or buildup: a coated appearance with visible material. It doesn't prove follicular blockage or poor hygiene — after reviewing sensitivity and skin integrity, it may support careful cleansing.
>
> Similar image, different possible story: the oil-dominant and surface-residue examples can look alike side by side — the camera shows the material, the consultation helps explain it. The microscope can't determine composition from appearance alone. Before you call it oil, scale, or product buildup, ask: when was the hair last washed? What products go on at the root? Is there tightness, itching, burning, tenderness, or rapid oil return? Is it present across the whole scalp, or concentrated in one region? And does a gentle cleanse change what's visible? When two possible stories look similar, ask a better question instead of choosing a stronger label.

### M4-07 — 4.7 From image to decision
**SOURCE:** Section 4.7, all 4 decision categories. **VOICE:** Reference for all 4 in order.

> Section 4.7 — From image to decision. The image earns its place by changing what happens next. Assessment isn't a performance — it should support one of four professional decisions.
>
> Preserve: the surface is intact, the client's comfortable, nothing calls for correction. Avoid over-treating — follow the client's cosmetic goals.
>
> Modify conservatively: oil, scale, residue, or regional variation is visible, without broken skin, marked pain, drainage, or a concerning loss pattern. Adjust cleansing, exfoliation, temperature, pressure, and product load — conservatively. Module 5 develops these directions further.
>
> Avoid or pause an area: a localized region shows discomfort, pronounced color change, recent irritation, tenderness, or real uncertainty. Don't overwork it — simplify, omit the area, or postpone that part of the service.
>
> And stop and refer: the scalp shows nonintact, draining, pustular, severely painful, hot, swollen, rapidly changing, patchy, smooth, shiny, or scar-like findings. Don't continue a cosmetic service over the area — explain the limit of the assessment, and recommend medical evaluation.
>
> [slowly] The advanced decision isn't always a more complex treatment. Sometimes it's less treatment. Sometimes it's no treatment today.

### M4-08 — 4.8 When not to proceed
**SOURCE:** Section 4.8, all 4 scenarios + verbatim referral script + device-contamination note. **VOICE:** Reference for all 4 scenarios and the referral script.

> Section 4.8 — When not to proceed. The most important image may be the one that ends the service.
>
> Nonintact or draining skin — broken, bleeding, weeping, oozing, pustular, crusted, or visibly draining. Stop camera contact, and don't continue the cosmetic service over the area.
>
> Marked pain or an inflammatory appearance — heat, swelling, severe tenderness, intense burning, or pain that seems disproportionate. Don't massage through pain, and don't use the service to test whether the area improves.
>
> A concerning hair-loss pattern — rapidly changing density, patchy loss, smooth or shiny areas, scar-like appearance, or loss involving brows or lashes. Describe and document what's visible, without naming the condition, and recommend medical evaluation.
>
> And uncertainty outside cosmetic scope — a finding that doesn't fit a course example, or several warning signs overlapping. You don't need the diagnosis to know the service should pause.
>
> [slowly] Here's the referral script: "I'm seeing an area that should be medically evaluated before we continue a scalp service over it. I can document what is visible, but I can't determine the cause from a cosmetic assessment. Once it has been evaluated, we can decide what kind of service is appropriate."
>
> If the device contacted a draining, bleeding, or otherwise compromised area, remove it from service and follow the manufacturer's required cleaning and disinfection procedure before reuse.

### M4-09 — 4.9 Practitioner insight
**SOURCE:** Section 4.9, all 5 info-cards. **VOICE:** Reference for all 5 in order.

> Section 4.9 — Practitioner insight. The device can create the evidence you think you found.
>
> Pressure changes color: excess contact can blanch or redden the scalp. Capture the baseline before repeated contact alters it.
>
> A dirty lens creates a false story: haze, residue, oil, or scale on the device can appear to belong to the client. Inspect and prepare the lens before the scan.
>
> Products can imitate pathology: dry shampoo, fibers, root spray, oils, masks, and medicated products can change surface appearance. Ask before you interpret.
>
> One image is not the scalp: a dramatic crown image doesn't erase a calm hairline, and a clean hairline doesn't represent the hidden posterior scalp.
>
> And before-and-after requires matching conditions: different light, angle, pressure, magnification, or location can create a more dramatic "result" than the service actually did.
>
> [firmly] Microscopy doesn't become advanced when the image is dramatic. It becomes advanced when the collection method is consistent and the interpretation is restrained.

### M4-10 — 4.10 Common mistakes + Checkpoint 2 (`m4cp2`)
**SOURCE:** Section 4.10, all 6 mistake cards + checkpoint prompt. **VOICE:** Reference for all 6 mistakes.

> Section 4.10 — Common mistakes. What to unlearn before the camera makes it look convincing.
>
> Naming the scalp after one image — the fix: complete the five-point scan and compare distribution before forming a working service direction.
>
> Pressing for a clearer view — the fix: improve parting, stability, focus, lighting, and lens cleanliness, never pressure.
>
> Turning visible material into a cause — the fix: describe location, color, texture, distribution, and ask about product and wash history.
>
> Saving images without clear permission — the fix: separate consent for live view, record capture, teaching, marketing, and external sharing.
>
> Creating a misleading before-and-after — the fix: match region, magnification, light, pressure, and orientation.
>
> And performing an assessment that changes nothing — the fix: the image should support preserve, modify, avoid, pause, or refer. Otherwise, it's theater.
>
> [firmly] The microscope is not the skill. The collection method, the language, and the decision are the skill.
>
> Here's your second checkpoint. During the crown assessment, you see a cluster of raised lesions with visible fluid and crusting. The client says the area doesn't bother them and asks you to continue. Explain what you do with the microscope and the service, what you say to the client, and why.
>
> Take your time, and answer above.
