### M8-02 — Think in phases, not steps
**SOURCE:** section intro + all 7 `concept-card`s ("Seven phases, at a glance").
**VOICE:** Teaching for the framing, Reference for all 7 phase cards — named individually, in order, term plus sub-label plus full definition.

> Section — Think in phases, not steps. Steps make you robotic. Phases keep you in control.
>
> If you think in steps, you become rigid — focused on what's next rather than what's happening right now. If you think in phases, you stay aware of the full arc of the service. You know where you are, what the client is feeling, and what's coming — without losing presence.
>
> Here are the seven phases, at a glance.
>
> One — Entry and Regulation. Opening rituals, microscopy, and dry work. Settle the client. Establish rhythm. Begin connection. If you feel rushed here, the rest of the service will feel rushed too.
>
> Two — Immersion. Water and initial stimulation. Introduce water. Deepen relaxation. Temperature inconsistency or hesitation here breaks trust immediately.
>
> Three — Treatment Work. Exfoliation and scalp massage. This is one of the more demanding phases to execute well — continuous movement, even pressure, no stopping and starting. Pausing turns treatment into a sequence instead of an experience.
>
> Four — Expansion. Neck, shoulders, and extended work. Widen the experience. Release tension. Deepen relaxation. Stay grounded, connected, not rushed.
>
> Five — Reset and Cleanse. Rinse, shampoo, and treatment. Bring the scalp back to balance. Smooth transitions, consistent temperature. No break in rhythm.
>
> Six — Signature Moments. Waterfall, cooling, and hot towel. Create memorable peaks — controlled, intentional, slightly elevated from the rest of the service.
>
> And seven — Exit. Wrap, sit-up, and transition out. This is where most practitioners fail. Rushing this moment makes the entire service feel shorter and less valuable. Keep it gradual, calm, and complete.

### M8-03 — 8.1 Core and Extended
**SOURCE:** Section 8.1, both `format-card`s, info-card "Designed around the service, not the clock."
**VOICE:** Teaching for framing, Reference for both format cards (every bullet named) and the info-card.

> Section 8.1 — Core and Extended. Two A I M T reference protocols, one foundation.
>
> Core and Extended are A I M T reference protocols that show how a complete service contracts or expands within a deliberately designed structure — not just two numbers on a menu. The difference is service structure, not simply "less time" versus "more time." Core keeps the essential flow intact, with its scalable massage, bodywork, and sensory portions tighter. Extended directs its added time intentionally toward selected massage, bodywork, and treatment portions — not spread evenly across every step. The masterclass ahead demonstrates the Extended, ninety-minute reference.
>
> Two formats. Core Format: a sixty-minute A I M T reference protocol. A complete, condensed reference. The essential service flow preserved. Scalable massage, bodywork, and sensory portions tighter. No hand and forearm massage.
>
> Extended Format: a ninety-minute A I M T reference protocol. A more expansive reference. Added time directed intentionally, not spread evenly. Deeper massage, bodywork, and treatment portions. Hand and forearm massage included.
>
> [warmly] Here's something worth keeping in mind: Core and Extended are A I M T's current reference formats. Each is intentionally built as its own service structure — not a formula for simply speeding up or stretching every step. A shorter service may require a more streamlined protocol, with deliberate decisions about which components belong. A longer service should expand only where additional time genuinely improves the experience — massage, bodywork, processing, or selected sensory work. The goal is to decide where the time adds value, not force the protocol to fit a number. For this module, pacing stays anchored to the Core and Extended reference formats.

### M8-05 — Chapter 01: Opening Rituals + Microscopy
**SOURCE:** `M8_CHAPTERS[0]` — guidance, why, teach, notes, watchFor.
**VOICE:** Teaching for framing, Reference throughout — this chapter's client-facing scripts are near-verbatim.

> Chapter one — Opening Rituals and Microscopy. Here's how to do it.
>
> Help the client change into the spa wrap, robe, comfortable socks, and a place for jewelry — tell them exactly where to meet you when they're ready, rather than leaving them to guess. If your space allows, offer a caffeine-free tea or beverage while you consult.
>
> If the intake form hasn't already been completed, have the client complete it now so you can review allergies, sensitivities, contraindications, and preferences before beginning.
>
> Move into scalp microscopy. Move slowly and deliberately, holding the microscope still when you find an area you want the client to observe. Explain what you're seeing in simple, client-friendly language — even a healthy scalp deserves at least a couple of meaningful observations. Connect every observation to the treatment plan using three parts: what you observed, what you're going to do or use, and why.
>
> Move into the aromatherapy ritual — your first intentional physical connection with the client. Place one hand firmly and confidently on the shoulder and keep it there throughout the selection, providing a point of security while the client's eyes are closed. Introduce the three blends one at a time without revealing them, pausing between each; once all three are sampled, ask which they prefer and reveal the blend they selected.
>
> Once the opening rituals are complete, let the client relax with their beverage while you prepare the treatment room — prefill the basin and wrap the faucet securely in a towel to weigh it down, keep it in place, and minimize noise later. Then bring the client in to begin the hands-on portion of the service.
>
> Why we do it: the opening rituals make the client feel comfortable, establish trust, and set the slower pace for the service before anything hands-on begins. A microscopy observation only becomes useful to the client when it's connected to the treatment plan — what you saw, what you're going to do, and why — never presented as an isolated finding. The sustained shoulder contact through aromatherapy is the deliberate bridge from no touch into hands-on treatment, and letting the client sample and choose their own blend turns the moment into genuine participation rather than a decision made for them.
>
> How to communicate it. Communication cue, changing room: "You'll find fuzzy socks on top if you'd like to take your shoes off and get comfortable. Those are yours to take home. Underneath is your spa wrap. You'll remove your shirt and bra, and the wrap goes straight across your chest like a strapless dress. Your robe is underneath to keep you comfortable during the consultation. I recommend removing necklaces so we have full access to the neck and shoulders during your massage."
>
> Example, a microscopy observation connected to the plan: "I'm seeing some sebum buildup around the follicles, so today we'll use a gentle exfoliation step with steam to help loosen that buildup, followed by a balancing hair bath to help regulate sebum production, and a strengthening mask through your ends to help alleviate further breakage."
>
> Communication cue, aromatherapy: "I'm going to have you sample three essential oil blends. Whichever one you're most drawn to is the blend we'll use throughout your service today. I'm going to have you close your eyes so you can focus completely on the scent."
>
> One note: touch expectations for this first-contact moment are established at intake and covered in depth in Module 2 — this is where you put that understanding into practice with genuine attentiveness, not where you ask for it from scratch.
>
> [slowly] Watch for in the demonstration: the shift from changing-room logistics into scalp microscopy, and how a shoulder-anchored touch carries through the full aromatherapy selection before the hands-on treatment begins.

### M8-08 — Chapter 05: Exfoliant + Scalp Massage
**SOURCE:** `M8_CHAPTERS[4]`.
**VOICE:** Teaching for framing, Reference throughout — this is the chapter `m8cp1` evaluates directly, so nothing here is thinned.

> Chapter five — Exfoliant and Scalp Massage. How to do it. Apply exfoliant from the mixing bowl using an applicator brush. Work in four quadrants — frontal, temporal, crown, occipital.
>
> Exfoliation intensity, product, and technique should match what this client's scalp is telling you today. A stronger application is not the default you dial back only when there's a problem — it's one point on a range you select from every time.
>
> The heart of the service: systematic scalp and head massage. Cover all regions, vary pressure, work the occipital ridge, temples, and neck junction.
>
> Rinse the exfoliant thoroughly. Consistent water temperature throughout. Shower head angled slightly — never directly overhead.
>
> Why we do it: exfoliation gives you a deliberate pre-cleanse method for surface buildup — the real skill is adapting product, amount, intensity, pressure, and technique to intake, observation, and client response, not applying one fixed routine. Scalp massage is one of the anchors of the Head Spa service: its value comes from intentional rhythm, full coverage, continuity, and appropriate pressure — controlled technique, not casual rubbing, and not a specific physiological outcome.
>
> How to communicate it. Keep the flow quiet: do not automatically explain exfoliation while applying it — know the explanation in case the client asks. If they ask: "It's a scalp exfoliant. I'm adjusting the product, pressure, and technique based on what I'm seeing and how your scalp feels today." Communication cue, pressure, during massage: "Let me know anytime if you'd like more or less pressure." Keep the flow quiet for the rinse — no additional narration needed. If they ask: "I'm rinsing everything through now before we move into the next part of the service."
>
> One note: this is where Core and Extended diverge most. Know your pace for each reference format.
>
> Two adaptation principles for this chapter. Adapt by degree, not by switch: a strong approach that suits one scalp can be too much for another. Reduce pressure, choose a gentler product, or work more selectively by region — rather than skipping the step outright unless there's a genuine safety reason to omit it. Adapt to response, not habit: pressure and intensity should be checked against client response, not applied uniformly regardless of what this client's scalp presentation and comfort are telling you in the moment.
>
> [slowly] Watch for in the demonstration: how exfoliation technique and intensity are selected for this client rather than applied on autopilot, how pressure and rhythm shift across regions during the massage that follows, and the clean rinse that closes the sequence out.

### M8-11 — Chapter 09: Final Rinse + Halo Massage, part 1 (guidance + why)
**SOURCE:** `M8_CHAPTERS[8]` — guidance (all 6 paragraphs) + why (both paragraphs). Split from part 2 for generation-length only; no content boundary is crossed.

> Chapter nine — Final Rinse and Halo Massage. How to do it.
>
> Before the final rinse, use a wide-tooth comb through the scalp for stimulation only — not detangling. Check water temperature on your own skin first, confirm it with the client, then rinse thoroughly from underneath, paying particular attention to the nape, around the ears, the front hairline, and the crown.
>
> Prepare the Halo with only enough clean water to run the flow. Protect the client's chest with a dry towel, position the Halo carefully, and keep the flow low. Continue into slow, broad neck-and-shoulder massage with steady contact so this portion feels like a gradual release from the treatment rather than an abrupt ending.
>
> As the Halo work ends, turn the water fully off and prevent dripping before returning the Halo to rest. Fold the protective towel back over the client and continue with slow, even, large movements.
>
> Choose one finishing element: a cooling scalp spray, or a warm towel infused with the client's chosen essential oil. If using the warm towel, test the temperature on your own skin before applying it.
>
> Gently squeeze — never rub — excess moisture from the hair, wrap it loosely to one side, remove the weighted blanket and eye mask, and help the client sit up slowly. Step out to give them privacy to dress.
>
> When they return, greet them with a glass of ice water and revisit the microscope so they can see their scalp after the service. Offer a blow-dry using controlled airflow and moderate heat, then finish with any final service observations or recommendations before moving into checkout.
>
> Why we do it: this phase clears the conditioning portion while gradually transitioning the client out of treatment. The final Halo work should remain broad, steady, and unhurried. The finishing element provides a deliberate sensory close, while the slower sit-up, privacy handoff, and re-greet prevent the transition out of the treatment room from feeling abrupt.
>
> Returning to the microscope gives the client a clear visual reference for what you observed after the service. The physical close should feel as intentional as the opening.

### M8-13 — 8.3 Flow, pressure & transitions
**SOURCE:** Section 8.3, all 4 info-cards, closing key-point.
**VOICE:** Teaching for framing, Reference for all 4 cards — named individually, full text, real order.

> Section 8.3 — Flow, pressure, and transitions. The skills most people never train explicitly.
>
> Flow control. Flow is what turns technique into experience. A strong service has no long pauses, no searching, no abrupt transitions. Ask yourself during service: am I stopping anywhere? Am I reaching for something? Am I thinking too hard about what's next? If yes — flow is breaking. Fix it by thinking one step ahead, positioning tools correctly before you need them.
>
> Pressure consistency. Clients don't analyze pressure. They feel inconsistency. Consistent pressure reads as intentional. Uneven or unpredictable pressure reads as uncertain — even when the underlying technique is sound. Consistency, not any single pressure level, is what a client actually feels as skill — an often-underestimated part of the service.
>
> Temperature control. You do not guess temperature. You confirm it. Every time. Water that is too hot, too cold, or inconsistent is not a small mistake — it is a trust break. Equipment fails. Your hand is the safeguard. Always test before contact.
>
> Transitions. Transitions are where services succeed or fail. A good transition feels like one movement leads into the next — no pause, no reset. A bad transition feels like stop, reposition, start again. That breaks immersion immediately. Fix it by thinking ahead and staying one step mentally ahead of your hands.
>
> [slowly] Pressure test your service: after practicing, ask — where did I hesitate? Where did flow break? Did anything feel disconnected? Did I reach for anything mid-service? Fix those points specifically. That is how you improve.

### M8-14 — Signature interaction: "Protect the Flow"
**SOURCE:** interaction intro, all 3 scenarios, all 9 option/feedback pairs (`M8_PROTECT_ANSWERS`), closing key-point.
**VOICE:** Teaching for framing, Reference for all scenario setups and every option/feedback pair — narrated in full, not just the correct answer, same precedent as Modules 5 and 6's signature interactions.

> Signature interaction — Protect the Flow. The plan changes mid-service more often than a beginner expects. Here are three moments where it does — decide how you'd preserve the service's flow and experience. This one's ungraded; nothing here is saved or scored.
>
> Scenario one. A client who opted into fragrance at the start of the service now asks, midway through, to skip it going forward. Three ways you might respond.
>
> Explain that the fragrance was already chosen together and continue as planned: holding the original plan treats a client's changed mind as an inconvenience rather than a preference. The scent was never the point of the service — the client's comfort is.
>
> Adjust without treating it as a disruption — acknowledge briefly and move on fragrance-free: this preserves the flow. A brief acknowledgment and moving on fragrance-free keeps the client from feeling like they disrupted anything. This is the response that protects the flow.
>
> Pause the service to ask several follow-up questions about why they changed their mind: a short follow-up is fine if genuinely needed, but several questions mid-service turns a simple preference change into an interrogation and breaks the relaxation experience.
>
> Scenario two. You're moving into Step four. The scalp presentation in front of you tells you a strong exfoliation approach would be too aggressive today. Three ways you might respond.
>
> Skip exfoliation entirely and move to the next step: skipping exfoliation entirely isn't the same as adapting it. Unless there's a genuine safety or scope reason, some form of gentler exfoliation is usually still appropriate — this treats the decision as a switch instead of a dial.
>
> Reduce intensity, pressure, or product strength and adapt the method — without eliminating the step: this is the adjustment, not the elimination. Reducing intensity, choosing a gentler product, or changing method preserves the step while matching what this scalp is telling you today. This is the response that protects the flow.
>
> Apply the standard approach as planned since the client didn't say anything was wrong: applying the standard approach because the client stayed quiet ignores what you observed. The client isn't the one trained to flag this — you are.
>
> Scenario three. The conditioning treatment needs more processing time than the format allows for today's appointment. Three ways you might respond.
>
> Adjust the pacing for the rest of the service calmly, without visibly scrambling: adjusting pacing calmly for the rest of the service is exactly what keeps a plan change invisible to the client — the service still feels intentional, not improvised. This is the response that protects the flow.
>
> Cut the processing time short so the rest of the service stays exactly on schedule: cutting a step short to protect the schedule sacrifices the treatment itself for the appearance of being on time — the opposite trade-off from what the client is paying for.
>
> Stop and audibly recalculate the timing in front of the client: visibly recalculating in front of the client turns a routine adjustment into a moment of visible uncertainty, which breaks the experience more than the time change itself would.
>
> [slowly] The protocol gives structure. Judgment keeps it appropriate.
