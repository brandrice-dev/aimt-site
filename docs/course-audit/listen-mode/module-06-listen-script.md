## The script

### M6-01 — Module Briefing (spoken)
**VOICE:** Teaching.

> Welcome to Module 6: Conditions and Disorders. Before you treat, you have to interpret correctly. By this point, you know how to observe the scalp and recognize scalp types. Now you're adding another layer — conditions that change how those patterns behave — because if you misread what you're seeing, you'll choose the wrong approach with confidence.
>
> Here's what's ahead: you'll learn what you can — and cannot — conclude from appearance alone, understand the most important distinction in scalp condition care, see how a single spectrum runs from Malassezia to seborrheic dermatitis, know exactly when to pause a service or refer out, and learn what actually makes a condition worse, so you never do it.
>
> [warmly] Pay attention to this: the most dangerous mistake here isn't missing a condition. It's treating a guess like a diagnosis.

### M6-02 — 6.1 Your role here + 6.2 What you can and cannot conclude
**VOICE:** Teaching throughout.

> Section 6.1 — Your role here. Recognize. Understand. Adjust. Know when to stop.
>
> Most clients are not intentionally mismanaging their scalp. They are guessing. They see flakes and assume dryness. They feel itching and assume dandruff. They see oil and assume they need stronger cleansing. Then they treat based on symptoms instead of cause. Your job is not to agree with their diagnosis. Your job is to interpret.
>
> In a head spa setting, you will regularly encounter clients with visible scalp concerns — flaking, oil imbalance, irritation, buildup, itching, discomfort. Some of these fall well within scope. Others require referral. Your responsibility is to recognize what you're seeing, understand what may be driving it, adjust your service, and know when not to proceed.
>
> Section 6.2 — What you can and cannot conclude from appearance alone. Appearance is suggestive. It is not conclusive.
>
> Flake color, size, oiliness, and redness tell you something — but not everything. Several presentations can look similar at a glance: dry scalp, early dandruff, ordinary product buildup, and — at the more involved end — presentations that resemble other conditions entirely, such as psoriasis, tinea capitis, or contact or atopic dermatitis. A cosmetic assessment cannot rule these look-alikes out.
>
> This module does not teach you to name or rule out those other conditions. It teaches you that they exist, so a confident-sounding visual read never becomes more certain than it actually is.
>
> [firmly] When a presentation is ambiguous or mixed, the responsible move is to ask about history — product use, wash timing, recent changes, symptoms — and favor the gentler service direction. Don't force a label to make the picture feel resolved.

### M6-03 — 6.3 The most important distinction in scalp care
**SOURCE:** both comparison cards (labels, tags, all bullets), both "deeper picture" details, and the "When it's not clean-cut" info-card — full text, no compression. **VOICE:** Teaching for the intro; Reference for both comparison profiles and the info-card.

> Section 6.3 — The most important distinction in scalp care. Dry scalp and dandruff are not the same thing.
>
> They may both present with flaking. But the cause — and therefore the treatment approach — is completely different. Getting this wrong is one of the most common and most consequential mistakes in scalp care. There's an illustration on screen comparing the two patterns side by side, and two cards below it you can tap to compare directly.
>
> Dry scalp — a hydration issue. Small, white, powdery flakes. A matte, non-oily surface. Tightness or mild itch. Minimal visible oil.
>
> Here's the deeper picture on dry scalp: it flakes because it lacks moisture. The hydrolipid film is depleted or compromised — the scalp isn't retaining what it needs. This is a barrier issue, not a disease process. Causes include aging, seasonal changes, over-cleansing, harsh products, and chemical processing. Treatment direction: gentle exfoliation to lift dead cells, then hydration and barrier repair. The goal is to restore what's missing, not suppress a biological process.
>
> Dandruff — a biological imbalance. Larger, yellowish flakes. Oily or clumped near the root. Visible oil at the scalp. Irritation or redness.
>
> Here's the deeper picture on dandruff: it's generally associated with the scalp environment shifting out of balance — excess oil, Malassezia yeast activity, inflammation, and faster skin-cell turnover typically combine to produce the flaking and irritation, though the exact balance varies from client to client. This is not a hydration problem — adding moisture to an oily, inflamed scalp can make it worse. Treatment direction: cleansing support, oil control, anti-fungal supporting ingredients, anti-inflammatory botanicals. The goal is to rebalance the environment, not repair a barrier.
>
> [slowly] When it's not clean-cut: some clients show features of both — moderate oil with some tightness, or flakes that don't clearly match either column. When that happens, don't force a label. Ask about recent products, wash frequency, and how long the pattern has been present, and favor the gentler service direction until the picture is clearer.

### M6-04 — 6.4 The cycle worth understanding
**SOURCE:** all 6 "Follow the cycle" steps, full text, no compression. **VOICE:** Teaching for the intro; Reference for the 6 steps; first-person for the Cadence note.

> Section 6.4 — The cycle worth understanding. Why the wrong product makes things worse.
>
> This is a common, avoidable pattern: a client sees flakes, assumes dandruff, and reaches for the strongest anti-dandruff product they can find. Here's what actually happens, in order — there's an interactive walk-through on screen if you want to open each stage yourself.
>
> One: the client notices flaking on their clothing and scalp. This is where it starts. A client sees flakes and immediately interprets them as dandruff. The assumption is almost universal — and almost always made without any real assessment.
>
> Two: they assume it must be dandruff and buy anti-dandruff shampoo. The product choice follows the assumption. Anti-dandruff shampoos are heavily marketed, widely available, and seem like the logical solution. The mistake is reaching for the wrong one.
>
> Three: the anti-dandruff shampoo strips the scalp of its remaining moisture and oils. Anti-dandruff shampoos strip oil, suppress yeast, and slow cell turnover. On a scalp that already lacks moisture, they remove what little barrier is left.
>
> Four: the scalp becomes drier, flaking worsens, and there's more irritation. The scalp responds to barrier loss by becoming drier, tighter, and more reactive. Flaking increases. The client interprets this as the condition getting worse.
>
> Five: the client increases usage, thinking they need more of the same product. The logical response to a product not working is to use more of it. This accelerates the damage cycle.
>
> Six: back to step one. The cycle continues, and they arrive at your table having over-treated for months. By the time they reach you, the scalp has been compromised repeatedly. Your first job is often to stop the damage, not start a new treatment.
>
> [warmly] Here's a note from me: this is not treatment failure. It's misidentification. Flaking alone does not justify anti-dandruff treatment. Your job is to identify what type of flaking you're seeing before recommending anything. Sometimes the best thing you can do is simplify — not add more.

### M6-05 — Real-time scenarios + Final reasoning
**SOURCE:** all 3 real-time scenarios, all 4 fields each; the "Where do you break the cycle?" final-reasoning prompt and all 3 options with their exact feedback text — see audit's Judgment call note. **VOICE:** Reference throughout.

> What this looks like in real time — three scenarios.
>
> Scenario one. Presentation: fine white flakes, no visible oil, tight matte scalp. Likely direction: a dry-scalp pattern. What this changes: this does not point you toward a stronger dandruff response — treating an already dry presentation as an oil-driven flaking pattern can intensify dryness and reactivity. Service direction: support the barrier, reduce stripping, and simplify the routine.
>
> Scenario two. Presentation: yellowish flakes, oil at the root, slight redness. Likely direction: a dandruff-spectrum pattern. What this changes: this pattern supports a cleansing-and-regulation direction rather than a hydration-first direction. Service direction: support cleansing, regulation, and an appropriate within-scope home-care direction.
>
> Scenario three. Presentation: flakes, some oil, mild irritation, not clean-cut. Likely direction: a mixed or unclear presentation. What this changes: this is where practitioners can overcorrect — when the pattern is mixed, the priority is not choosing the strongest response, it's avoiding an aggressive one before the picture is clearer. Service direction: favor balance first, avoid aggressive stripping, and ask more questions and choose the gentler direction until the pattern is clearer.
>
> Final reasoning: where do you break the cycle? A client arrives after repeatedly escalating anti-dandruff products for a dry, increasingly reactive scalp. What should change first? There are three choices on screen. I'll walk through all three.
>
> First option, move to a stronger dandruff product — this keeps the cycle going. Escalating to a stronger product without reassessing the presentation is the same assumption that produced this client's reactive scalp in the first place.
>
> Second option, reassess the presentation before choosing the product direction — this breaks the cycle. The practitioner stops treating the client's original assumption as the answer, reassesses the presentation, and chooses the gentlest appropriate service and product direction from there.
>
> Third option, add more exfoliation — this keeps the cycle going. Adding exfoliation increases intensity before you've established whether the scalp needs it — reassess the presentation first.
>
> [firmly] Across all three, the same lesson holds: the presentation always gets reassessed before the product direction changes. Escalating blind is what created the cycle in the first place.

### M6-08 — 6.5 Malassezia to seborrheic dermatitis: one spectrum
**SOURCE:** all 4 spectrum stage descriptions, full text, no compression. **VOICE:** Teaching for the intro; Reference for the 4 stages.

> Section 6.5 — Malassezia to seborrheic dermatitis: one spectrum. Not two conditions. One spectrum.
>
> Malassezia is a naturally occurring yeast found on most scalps. It becomes a problem when several factors line up together — increased oil, a shift in the scalp's surface environment, and individual variation in how the scalp's immune and inflammatory response reacts to the yeast's activity. As it progresses, it moves along a spectrum — the same underlying process, at different levels of severity. There's an interactive slider on screen with four positions. Here's what each one looks like.
>
> Balanced: a balanced scalp has Malassezia present at normal levels — no visible flaking, no redness, no irritation. Sebum production is balanced.
>
> Mild dandruff: early Malassezia-driven dandruff shows small white to yellow flakes, slight clustering, mild oil, minimal redness. Still within scope with appropriate product choices.
>
> Moderate: at the moderate stage, flakes are more visible, oil presence is notable, and there's mild redness. The client likely notices this daily. A head spa can support this — consistent home care is also needed.
>
> Seborrheic dermatitis: this stage shows larger, thicker flakes, pronounced redness, and flaking that can spread beyond the scalp to the eyebrows, hairline, or ears. We'll cover exactly when to pause or refer next.

### M6-09 — 6.6 When to pause or refer
**SOURCE:** all 5 referral criteria, verbatim referral script. **VOICE:** Teaching for the intro; Reference for the 5 criteria; the script delivered as a verbatim quote.

> Section 6.6 — When to pause or refer. Know these without needing to touch anything.
>
> Refer when you see: redness or scaling that spreads beyond the scalp — to the eyebrows, hairline, or ears — combined with active irritation. Broken, weeping, crusted, or bleeding skin. Symptoms the client describes as severe, painful, or rapidly worsening. No improvement despite reasonable home care and product changes. Or any presentation you are not confident matches a typical dry-scalp or dandruff-spectrum pattern — including anything that could resemble a different condition, the way we covered back in 6.2.
>
> [slowly] Here's the referral script: "I'm seeing something today that goes beyond what a head spa service is designed to address. I don't want to guess at the cause or risk making it worse, so I'd recommend having it evaluated before we continue with scalp services."

### M6-10 — Signature interaction: Sort three presentations
**SOURCE:** all 3 presentations verbatim; all 3 sort options and all 9 option/feedback pairs from `M6_SORT_ANSWERS` — see audit's Judgment call note. **VOICE:** Reference throughout.

> Signature interaction: sort three presentations. For each presentation, decide whether to proceed as usual, proceed with a modification, or pause and refer. Weigh the combination of cues together — not any single feature in isolation. There are three options for each one: proceed as usual, proceed with modification, or pause and refer. I'll walk through all three for each presentation.
>
> Presentation one: fine white powdery flakes, matte surface, minimal oil, mild tightness, no redness.
> Proceed as usual — not quite: treating this as your standard service overlooks the barrier-supportive adjustments a dry-scalp presentation like this one calls for. Proceed with modification — correct: the matte, powdery, minimal-oil presentation supports a dry-scalp-pattern direction — a modified, hydration-supportive routine, with simplification if the client reports over-treating. Pause and refer — not quite: nothing described here — no redness, no spread, no severe discomfort — meets a referral trigger. This presentation can be served with adjustment, not paused.
>
> Presentation two: yellowish, clumped flakes, visible oil at the root, mild redness, contained to the scalp, no spread.
> Proceed as usual — not quite: visible oil and mild redness call for cleansing support and an OTC-category recommendation, not the unmodified standard service. Proceed with modification — correct: oil, clumped yellowish flakes, and redness contained to the scalp support a modified, cleansing-forward direction with an OTC-category anti-fungal or zinc recommendation, plus monitoring. Pause and refer — not quite: the redness here is mild and contained to the scalp, with no spread — that doesn't meet the threshold for pausing the service and referring out.
>
> Presentation three: scale and redness spreading to the eyebrows and hairline, the client reports significant discomfort, and the pattern has persisted for months despite drugstore treatments.
> Proceed as usual — not quite: spread beyond the scalp with active redness and significant discomfort are referral-relevant findings — this isn't a standard-service presentation. Proceed with modification — not quite: a modified service still assumes proceeding is appropriate. Spread to the eyebrows and hairline, combined with significant discomfort and months of unresolved symptoms, calls for stepping back from the service, not adjusting it. Pause and refer — correct: spread beyond the scalp with active redness, real discomfort, and months without improvement despite home treatment are referral-relevant findings — this is a pause-and-refer decision, not a service one.
>
> [firmly] Across all three, the same read applies: weigh redness, spread, discomfort, and duration together, and let the combination — not any one feature — decide whether you proceed, modify, or refer.

### M6-11 — 6.7 Treatment within scope
**SOURCE:** all 4 treatment cards + "product category is not a diagnosis" note, full text. **VOICE:** Teaching for the intro; Reference for all 4 cards and the info-card.

> Section 6.7 — Treatment within scope. You are supporting the environment. Not treating the condition.
>
> Cleansing support: regular cleansing reduces excess oil, buildup, and the microbial overgrowth that Malassezia feeds on. Consistency matters more than intensity.
>
> Anti-fungal supporting ingredients: zinc pyrithione, 1%-strength ketoconazole, and 1% selenium sulfide are OTC categories that help manage yeast activity. Never recommend or name a prescription-strength, 2%, product.
>
> Anti-inflammatory botanicals: licorice root and chamomile calm the scalp — reducing redness, irritation, and discomfort without disrupting the barrier. Preferred over aggressive treatments in a head spa context.
>
> And regular maintenance: irregular care leads to fluctuation in symptoms. The most effective approach is consistent support over time — not aggressive intervention when things flare.
>
> [warmly] A product category is not a diagnosis. Recommending one is a retail suggestion within a cosmetic service — never a diagnosis, never a prescription. If a presentation meets any of the referral criteria we just covered, refer instead of recommending a product.

### M6-12 — 6.8 What makes it worse
**SOURCE:** all 4 trigger cards, full text, no compression; the "Real-world integration" note; the "Practitioner insight" Cadence card. **VOICE:** Teaching for the intro; Reference for the 4 triggers and both cards.

> Section 6.8 — What makes it worse. Understanding what aggravates these conditions helps you educate clients in a way that actually changes their behavior.
>
> Stress and hormonal changes: stress and hormonal shifts are associated with flare-ups of existing conditions, most likely through a combination of immune and inflammatory response and changes in oil production — not oil production alone. Clients who notice seasonal or cyclical worsening are often seeing a hormonal pattern. Asking about stress levels and lifestyle during intake is useful context, not a diagnosis.
>
> Diet high in sugar, fried, or inflammatory foods: this may contribute for some clients, though the evidence here is largely individual rather than firmly established. It's worth asking about as part of intake — not something to lecture the client about or present as a guaranteed fix.
>
> Excess oil production: this creates a favorable environment for Malassezia overgrowth. Warmer weather, humidity, and hormonal shifts are commonly associated with a client noticing more oil between washes, though the relationship isn't precise or predictable enough to apply as a fixed rule for any individual client. Ask about recent heat exposure, exercise, and seasonal changes as part of intake rather than assuming a specific cause.
>
> And wrong product use: this is the most correctable trigger. Overly harsh or stripping products worsen both dryness and dandruff conditions. Clients often arrive already over-treating — multiple shampoos, aggressive products, inconsistent routines. Simplification is often more effective than adding more.
>
> Real-world integration, layering this on Module 5: this module does not replace scalp-type analysis. It layers on top of it. You are now asking: is this dry? Is this oil-driven? Is this microbial? Is this mixed? And then — what matters most right now? The practitioner who can answer those questions quickly and correctly is the one who makes decisions instead of guesses.
>
> [warmly] Something I've noticed along the way: after enough clients, you start to notice that most people are treating the wrong problem. They over-cleanse, over-treat, constantly switch products. Sometimes the best move is not adding more. It's simplifying. Correct interpretation always comes before correct treatment.
