Section 7.3 — Station prep sequence. Run this before every single client.

On screen, a station set up in reach order — the items used most, kept closest.

Inconsistency in setup is invisible to you and obvious to your client. A client who experienced a perfectly set room last visit notices when the towel warmer is cold or the cart is in the wrong place — even if they can't articulate why the experience felt off. Build a prep sequence and run it the same way every time.

The order below isn't arbitrary — it follows a build sequence: sanitation and structural setup first, then product and tool staging, then comfort, then ambiance, so nothing has to be assembled while the room is being sanitized or the client is already in it.

One: halo flush. A Barbicide rinse, then plain water until clear.

Two: product dishes. Three bowls — shampoo, mask, exfoliant — with the applicator brush alongside.

Three: towel warmer loaded. Confirm it's warm to the touch before the client arrives.

Four: fresh bed linens. Sheet, headrest cover, and a clean pillow if used.

Five: bed warmer on, set to an appropriate temperature.

Six: room temperature, set lower than normal, since the client will be wet.

Seven: spa wrap, robe, and slipper socks, folded and ready at the station entry.

Eight: a towel wrapped around the halo nozzle hose. It quiets it, prevents spray, and maintains atmosphere.

Nine: massage oil and lotion, ready on the cart and easily accessible.

And ten: ambient — music, lighting, diffuser — running before the client enters the room.

Section 7.4 — Client positioning. Three things to confirm before the water runs.

On screen, two side-by-side photos. The first: correct — occipital supported, neck relaxed, shoulders clear of the edge. The second: incorrect — chin lifted, neck extended over the basin edge. That's the exact position to avoid.

Positioning errors are hard to correct mid-service without breaking the experience. Get these right before you start — it takes thirty seconds and prevents a disruption you cannot undo.

One: halo alignment. The client centered under the halo so water hits evenly across the scalp. Adjust the client's position — not the halo.

Two: shoulder position. The top of the shoulders sits one to two inches off the edge of the bed. This gives you clean access to the neck without the client sliding.

Three: occipital support. The occipital bone rests in the deepest curve of the headrest, with the neck naturally relaxed — not extended, not flexed. If the chin is lifting, reposition.

Why this matters: a relaxed, neutral neck position isn't just about comfort — sustained hyperextension against a hard basin edge is a documented, if rare, safety concern. Keeping the neck supported and unextended, as the three checks above describe, is the setup detail most directly within a practitioner's control to reduce that risk — not a guarantee against it.

[firmly] Watch for: the client reports neck strain, dizziness, or discomfort at any point, or the chin is visibly lifting. Some discomfort signals are only about comfort; if a client ever reports dizziness, visual changes, or slurred speech, stop immediately and treat it as a medical concern, not a positioning fix.

What to do: stop the service, adjust the positioning or temperature issue, communicate what you're doing and check in with the client, then resume only once she confirms she's comfortable. Never adjust silently or push through to a natural stopping point.

[slowly] Here's a note from me: cover the client first, then slide the knee bolster into place. Weighted blanket across the chest if using. Eye mask on. Then begin. Doing it out of sequence makes the client feel arranged rather than cared for.

Pressure test your setup: ask yourself — if something unexpected happens, can I adjust without breaking flow? Can I reach everything without stepping away? Does this still work when I'm not thinking perfectly? If not, your setup is not ready.
