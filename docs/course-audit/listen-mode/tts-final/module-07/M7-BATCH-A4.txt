Now for a signature interaction: find the setup mistakes. Not every difference is a mistake. Look at each setup and decide whether something actually needs to change — or whether it's simply a different, functional way of working.

Setup one: the cart is positioned on the practitioner's left. Does anything here actually need to change? Acceptable variation. Different does not automatically mean wrong — the cart can sit on either side as long as active-use items remain comfortably reachable and the setup supports efficient service flow. Reachability matters. Left versus right does not.

Setup two: the items used throughout the service are positioned well outside the immediate working area. Does this setup need correction? Yes — needs correction. Frequently used items belong within comfortable working reach. Repeatedly stepping away for active-use products interrupts service flow and signals that the station needs reorganizing. Frequency of use should influence placement.

Setup three: this treatment bed does not have optional armrests. Is that itself a setup problem? Not necessarily — acceptable variation. Equipment should be judged by function, support, and service usability — not by whether it includes every optional feature. Armrests may be a practitioner preference rather than a universal requirement. Equipment preference is not the same as a functional requirement.

And setup four: look at the client's head, neck, and shoulder position. Does this setup need correction? Yes — needs correction. The client is positioned too far from the head end, leaving the occipital area poorly supported and the neck extended. Reposition the client before continuing. What to remember: professional setup is judged by function — support, reach, comfort, and service flow — not by whether every treatment room looks identical.
