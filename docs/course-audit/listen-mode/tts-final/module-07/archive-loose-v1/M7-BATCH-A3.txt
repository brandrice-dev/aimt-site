Section 7.4 — Client positioning. Three things to confirm before the water runs.

Positioning errors are hard to correct mid-service without breaking the experience — get these right before you start. It takes thirty seconds and prevents a disruption you can't undo.

Halo alignment: client centered under the halo so water hits evenly. Adjust the client's position, not the halo. Shoulder position: top of shoulders one to two inches off the edge of the bed, for clean neck access without sliding. And occipital support: the occipital bone rests in the deepest curve of the headrest, neck naturally relaxed — not extended, not flexed. If the chin is lifting, reposition.

[firmly] A relaxed, neutral neck position isn't just comfort — sustained hyperextension against a hard basin edge is a documented, if rare, safety concern. Keeping the neck supported is the setup detail most directly within your control to reduce that risk, not a guarantee against it.

Watch for: the client reports neck strain, dizziness, or discomfort at any point, or the chin is visibly lifting. Some signals are only about comfort — but if a client ever reports dizziness, visual changes, or slurred speech, stop immediately and treat it as a medical concern, not a positioning fix. What to do: stop the service, adjust the issue, communicate what you're doing and check in, then resume only once she confirms she's comfortable. Never adjust silently or push through to a "natural" stopping point.

[slowly] Cover the client first, then slide the knee bolster into place. Weighted blanket across the chest if using. Eye mask on. Then begin. Doing it out of sequence makes the client feel arranged rather than cared for.

Pressure test your setup: if something unexpected happens, can you adjust without breaking flow? Can you reach everything without stepping away? Does it still work when you're not thinking perfectly? If not, your setup isn't ready.

Signature interaction: find the setup mistakes. Not every difference is a mistake — for each setup on screen, decide whether something actually needs to change, or whether it's simply a different, functional way of working.

Setup one: the cart's positioned on the practitioner's left. Different doesn't automatically mean wrong — the cart can sit on either side as long as active-use items stay comfortably reachable. Reachability matters; left versus right doesn't.

Setup two: items used throughout the service are positioned well outside the immediate working area. This one needs correction — frequently used items belong within comfortable reach. Repeatedly stepping away interrupts flow and signals the station needs reorganizing.

Setup three: the treatment bed has no optional armrests. Not necessarily a problem — equipment gets judged by function, support, and usability, not by whether it includes every optional feature. Preference isn't the same as a functional requirement.

And setup four: look at the client's head, neck, and shoulder position. This needs correction — positioned too far from the head end, occipital poorly supported, neck extended. Reposition before continuing.

[firmly] Professional setup is judged by function — support, reach, comfort, service flow — not by whether every treatment room looks identical.
