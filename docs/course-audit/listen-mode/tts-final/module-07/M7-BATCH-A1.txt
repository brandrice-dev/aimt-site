Section 7.1 — The treatment bed. Your most important equipment decision.

On screen, a fully configured head spa bed, ready for service.

The head spa bed is the foundation of everything. Comfort, positioning, water flow, massage access, client perception — it all builds from this. You can perform a service without the right bed. But you cannot create a premium experience without one.

A halo-equipped wet bed is the foundation of a true head spa service. The halo allows water immersion during massage, which changes the client's physical and psychological state in a way that dry work cannot replicate. Evaluate any bed you're considering against what it actually needs to accomplish — not against how it looks in a photo.

What most people get wrong: they choose based on how it looks, not how it performs. A bed with restrictive armrests may photograph beautifully — but larger clients feel confined, taller clients feel compressed, and movement becomes limited. Once comfort breaks, the entire service is affected. Nothing will compensate for it.

Evaluate the bed against what it needs to do — not how it looks. Run any bed you're offered or can afford against these seven categories.

One: basin and head support. The headrest curve should actually support the occipital bone in a relaxed, non-extended position — this connects directly to correct client positioning in Section 7.4.

Two: client entry, exit, and stability. A client should be able to get on and off the bed safely, and the bed shouldn't shift or wobble under normal movement.

Three: practitioner reach and working height. The bed's height and access affect your own posture and reach across an entire shift — not just a single service.

Four: water management. How water is contained and drained affects both service flow and how easily the station can be sanitized between clients.

Five: cleaning and sanitation compatibility. Surfaces and seams need to be things you can actually disinfect between clients — not just wipe at a glance.

Six: space requirements — a preference, not a universal standard. How much room a bed needs depends on your specific space and business. This is a practitioner-preference and business-context decision, not a documented requirement.

And seven: armrest configuration — a preference, not a requirement. Armrests that tuck in larger or taller clients are a commonly reported comfort complaint among experienced practitioners — a preference many share, not a documented safety or ergonomic requirement. The deciding factor is always how a given bed performs for your actual clientele.

There is no single required bed model — evaluating any bed against these categories, instead of how it photographs, is what makes the decision transferable to whatever you're actually offered or can afford.

Here's a note from me: a common early mistake is prioritizing what looks impressive in photos over what actually feels comfortable to lie in for an hour. Choose the bed your clients will want to come back to — not the one that photographs well.
