Signature interaction — Protect the Flow. The plan changes mid-service more often than a beginner expects. Here are three moments where it does — decide how you'd preserve the service's flow and experience. This one's ungraded; nothing here is saved or scored.

Scenario one. A client who opted into fragrance at the start of the service now asks, midway through, to skip it going forward. Three ways you might respond.

Explain that the fragrance was already chosen together and continue as planned: holding the original plan treats a client's changed mind as an inconvenience rather than a preference. The scent was never the point of the service — the client's comfort is.

Adjust without treating it as a disruption — acknowledge briefly and move on fragrance-free: this preserves the flow. A brief acknowledgment and moving on fragrance-free keeps the client from feeling like they disrupted anything. This is the response that protects the flow.

Pause the service to ask several follow-up questions about why they changed their mind: a short follow-up is fine if genuinely needed, but several questions mid-service turns a simple preference change into an interrogation and breaks the relaxation experience.

Scenario two. You're moving into Step four. The scalp presentation in front of you tells you a strong exfoliation approach would be too aggressive today. Three ways you might respond.

Skip exfoliation entirely and move to the next step: skipping exfoliation entirely isn't the same as adapting it. Unless there's a genuine safety or scope reason, some form of gentler exfoliation is usually still appropriate — this treats the decision as a switch instead of a dial.

Reduce intensity, pressure, or product strength and adapt the method — without eliminating the step: this is the adjustment, not the elimination. Reducing intensity, choosing a gentler product, or changing method preserves the step while matching what this scalp is telling you today. This is the response that protects the flow.

Apply the standard approach as planned since the client didn't say anything was wrong: applying the standard approach because the client stayed quiet ignores what you observed. The client isn't the one trained to flag this — you are.

Scenario three. The conditioning treatment needs more processing time than the format allows for today's appointment. Three ways you might respond.

Adjust the pacing for the rest of the service calmly, without visibly scrambling: adjusting pacing calmly for the rest of the service is exactly what keeps a plan change invisible to the client — the service still feels intentional, not improvised. This is the response that protects the flow.

Cut the processing time short so the rest of the service stays exactly on schedule: cutting a step short to protect the schedule sacrifices the treatment itself for the appearance of being on time — the opposite trade-off from what the client is paying for.

Stop and audibly recalculate the timing in front of the client: visibly recalculating in front of the client turns a routine adjustment into a moment of visible uncertainty, which breaks the experience more than the time change itself would.

[slowly] The protocol gives structure. Judgment keeps it appropriate.
