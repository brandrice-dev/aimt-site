Welcome to Module 6: Conditions and Disorders. Before you treat, you have to interpret correctly. By this point, you know how to observe the scalp and recognize scalp types. Now you're adding another layer — conditions that change how those patterns behave — because if you misread what you're seeing, you'll choose the wrong approach with confidence.

Here's what's ahead: you'll learn what you can — and cannot — conclude from appearance alone, understand the most important distinction in scalp condition care, see how a single spectrum runs from Malassezia to seborrheic dermatitis, know exactly when to pause a service or refer out, and learn what actually makes a condition worse, so you never do it.

[warmly] Pay attention to this: the most dangerous mistake here isn't missing a condition. It's treating a guess like a diagnosis.

Section 6.1 — Your role here. Recognize. Understand. Adjust. Know when to stop.

Most clients are not intentionally mismanaging their scalp. They are guessing. They see flakes and assume dryness. They feel itching and assume dandruff. They see oil and assume they need stronger cleansing. Then they treat based on symptoms instead of cause. Your job is not to agree with their diagnosis. Your job is to interpret.

In a head spa setting, you will regularly encounter clients with visible scalp concerns — flaking, oil imbalance, irritation, buildup, itching, discomfort. Some of these fall well within scope. Others require referral. Your responsibility is to recognize what you're seeing, understand what may be driving it, adjust your service, and know when not to proceed.

Section 6.2 — What you can and cannot conclude from appearance alone. Appearance is suggestive. It is not conclusive.

Flake color, size, oiliness, and redness tell you something — but not everything. Several presentations can look similar at a glance: dry scalp, early dandruff, ordinary product buildup, and — at the more involved end — presentations that resemble other conditions entirely, such as psoriasis, tinea capitis, or contact or atopic dermatitis. A cosmetic assessment cannot rule these look-alikes out.

This module does not teach you to name or rule out those other conditions. It teaches you that they exist, so a confident-sounding visual read never becomes more certain than it actually is.

[firmly] When a presentation is ambiguous or mixed, the responsible move is to ask about history — product use, wash timing, recent changes, symptoms — and favor the gentler service direction. Don't force a label to make the picture feel resolved.
