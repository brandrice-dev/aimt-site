Section 3.4 — The hair-growth cycle. Hair loss is often a timing problem before it's a pattern problem.

Every follicle cycles independently through growth, transition, rest, and release. That staggered timing is why normal shedding is continuous — and why a major event may not show up in the hair until months later.

Anagen, growth: the follicle actively produces the hair shaft. Scalp anagen lasts years, which is why scalp hair can grow long. The length of anagen varies — a surface service can't extend it on command.

Catagen, transition: the lower follicle regresses over a period of weeks, and the growing phase ends. Catagen is brief, and represents transition, not sudden surface damage.

Telogen, relative rest: the follicle stays in a resting phase for roughly several months before the retained club hair is released. A trigger that shifts more follicles into telogen may not produce visible shedding right away.

And exogen, release — often described as the shedding portion of the cycle. The club hair separates and sheds from the follicle. Shedding is a normal part of cycling; the pattern, amount, timing, duration, and accompanying signs are what determine whether it deserves closer evaluation.

[slowly] The event and the shedding may be separated by months. That delay is one of the most useful pieces of history a practitioner can recognize.

Predict before you reveal: a client has a high fever today. When could related shedding become noticeable? On screen you'll see three options — within the same week, about two to three weeks later, or roughly two to three months later. Think through what you just learned about the cycle, and make your prediction before moving on.
