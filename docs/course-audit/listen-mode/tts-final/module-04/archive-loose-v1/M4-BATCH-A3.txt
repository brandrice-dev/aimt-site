Section 4.4 — The five-point scalp scan. The scalp isn't uniform — A I M T's baseline scan uses the same five assessment points, in the same order, every time, so one dramatic image never gets to represent the entire head. The memory line: front, top, crown, side, back.

On screen there's a reference photo for each of the five stations — frontal hairline, top parting, crown and vertex, temporal area, and occipital, the back of the head. The frontal hairline is where styling products, tension, and scale are often easiest to notice. The top parting gives you a consistent central view of follicular openings and hair distribution. The crown gets compared against the parting and hairline rather than assumed to match. The temporal area lets you compare a lateral region against the center — check the opposite side if asymmetry or a localized concern comes up. And the occipital, the hidden back of the head, may look different from the areas the client sees and styles most often — position the hair securely so the lens stays steady.

A five-point scan creates the baseline. The client's concern tells you where to add targeted views.

Section 4.5 — The five observation lenses. Don't label the scalp — read the evidence. Use the same five lenses at every assessment area.

Scalp surface: color relative to the client's own baseline, sheen, loose or adherent scale, visible coating, whether the skin is intact. Document like this — "diffuse surface shine at the crown with small areas of adherent yellow-white material."

Follicular openings: whether they're visible, and whether material is present at or near them. Document like this — "several openings show visible material around the emerging shafts." Never write "the follicles are clogged."

Perifollicular area: color change, scale, or visible material immediately surrounding a follicle.

Hair shafts: visible breakage, coating, residue, variation in shaft appearance, how many shafts are in the field — but one image can't establish the cause of breakage or thinning.

And distribution: whether the finding is localized, diffuse, symmetric, or different from another assessment area. Distribution is often more useful than the most dramatic close-up — one image is a detail, the scan is the pattern.

Now — observation discipline. On screen, you'll classify five real statements as a supported observation, a working question, or an unsupported conclusion. Things like "the crown shows diffuse shine and visible yellow-white material" versus "this is seborrheic dermatitis." [firmly] Strong assessment language is precise about what's visible, and disciplined about what remains unknown.
