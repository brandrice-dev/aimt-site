Section 4.5 — The five observation lenses. Do not label the scalp. Read the evidence.

Use the same five lenses at every assessment area. They keep the assessment specific enough to be useful and restrained enough to remain honest.

Lens one — scalp surface. Look for color relative to the client's surrounding baseline, sheen, loose or adherent scale, visible coating, residue, and whether the skin is intact. Document it like this: "Diffuse surface shine at the crown with small areas of adherent yellow-white material."

Lens two — follicular openings. Look for whether openings are visible and whether material is present at or near them. Document it like this: "Several openings show visible material around the emerging shafts." And do not write: "The follicles are clogged."

Lens three — the perifollicular area. Look for color change, scale, or visible material immediately surrounding a follicle. Document it like this: "Color change and fine scale are visible around several follicles in this assessment area."

Lens four — hair shafts. Look for visible breakage, coating, residue, variation in shaft appearance, and the number of shafts visible in the field. Document it like this: "Short and longer shafts are visible in the same field; several shafts have surface residue." One limit: one image cannot establish the cause of breakage, thinning, or shaft variation.

Lens five — distribution. Look for whether the finding is localized, diffuse, symmetric, or different from another assessment area. Document it like this: "The crown shows more surface shine and residue than the frontal hairline."

Distribution is often more useful than the most dramatic close-up. One image is a detail. The scan is the pattern.

Observation discipline. Say only what the image earned.

This is a practice exercise — classify each statement as a supported observation, a working question, or an unsupported conclusion. Five statements to test your ear.

Statement one: "The crown shows diffuse shine and visible yellow-white material around several follicular openings." That's a supported observation — it describes location and appearance without claiming what the material is or why it is present.

Statement two: "Could the visible material be influenced by dry shampoo, root spray, oil, or wash timing?" That's a working question — it identifies missing context and directs the consultation without converting the image into a conclusion.

Statement three: "The client's follicles are clogged because they do not shampoo enough." That's an unsupported conclusion — the image cannot prove obstruction, hygiene, cause, or the client's cleansing behavior.

Statement four: "The temporal area looks darker and the client reports burning, so I would avoid stimulation there and ask whether this is new." That's a working question — the statement combines a visible difference with the client's report and changes the cosmetic service without naming a condition.

Statement five: "This is seborrheic dermatitis." That's an unsupported conclusion — a cosmetic magnification image does not establish a medical diagnosis.

Strong assessment language is precise about what is visible and disciplined about what remains unknown.
