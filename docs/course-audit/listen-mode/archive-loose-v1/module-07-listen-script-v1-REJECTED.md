## The script

### M7-01 — Module Briefing (spoken)
**VOICE:** Teaching.

> Welcome to Module 7. By the time your hands touch the client, they've already formed an impression — not consciously, but they feel whether you're prepared, whether the space is controlled, whether the service will be smooth or interrupted. Your setup isn't separate from the service. It's part of it.
>
> You'll set up a treatment bed that works for the service, know your tools and supplies well enough to prep without thinking twice, build a repeatable station-prep sequence you can trust under pressure, position a client for comfort, access, and safety, and practice the judgment calls that come up in real station setup.
>
> [warmly] Pay attention to this: a client can't see your prep, but they can feel it. A controlled setup is part of the service itself.

### M7-02 — 7.1 The treatment bed
**VOICE:** Reference for all 7 evaluation categories.

> Section 7.1 — The treatment bed. Your most important equipment decision.
>
> The bed is the foundation of everything — comfort, positioning, water flow, massage access, client perception, all of it builds from here. A halo-equipped wet bed is the foundation of a true head spa service; the halo allows water immersion during massage in a way dry work can't replicate.
>
> [firmly] What most people get wrong: they choose based on how it looks, not how it performs. A bed with restrictive armrests may photograph beautifully, but larger clients feel confined, taller clients feel compressed, movement becomes limited — and once comfort breaks, the entire service is affected.
>
> Evaluate any bed against what it needs to do, not how it looks, across seven categories. Basin and head support — the headrest curve should actually support the occipital bone in a relaxed position. Client entry, exit, and stability — safe on and off, no shift or wobble. Practitioner reach and working height — affects your posture across an entire shift, not just one service. Water management — how it's contained and drained affects flow and sanitation. Cleaning and sanitation compatibility — surfaces you can actually disinfect, not just wipe. And two preference categories, not universal requirements: space requirements, which depend on your specific business, and armrest configuration, a commonly reported comfort preference among practitioners, not a documented requirement.
>
> [slowly] There's no single required bed model. Evaluating any bed against these categories — instead of how it photographs — is what makes the decision transferable to whatever you're actually offered or can afford. A common early mistake is prioritizing what looks impressive in photos over what actually feels comfortable to lie in for an hour. Choose the bed your clients will want to come back to.

### M7-03 — 7.2 Tools and supplies
**VOICE:** Teaching, Reference for the 4 categories and 3 reach zones.

> Section 7.2 — Tools and supplies. Essentials first. Upgrades later.
>
> Most people overvalue tools — they assume more tools means a better service. Your hands create the experience. Tools support, enhance, and assist, but they should never replace presence. Overuse leads to a mechanical-feeling service and inconsistent pressure. Clients may not say it, but they feel it.
>
> On screen, four expandable categories separate what's essential from what's an upgrade: linens and comfort, service tools, sanitation supplies, and the ambient experience. Start with everything on the essentials list — nothing on the upgrade list is necessary to deliver a premium service. Add from the upgrade tier once your volume justifies it.
>
> Where something lives on your cart matters as much as whether you own it. Arrange by how often you reach for it during a service, not by those four buying categories. The within-reach zone, shoulder-to-waist height, no step required: your product dishes and applicator brush — everything you touch during nearly every step. The one-step zone: items used once or twice per service, like your comb or blow dryer. And the reserve zone, off the working surface entirely: backup linens, upgrade tools not in use, sanitation stock — keeping these off the working surface keeps your clean-and-dirty separation clean and visible during the live service, not just at setup.

### M7-04 — 7.3 Station prep sequence
**SOURCE:** all 10 prep steps, in order. **VOICE:** Reference for the sequence.

> Section 7.3 — Station prep sequence. Run this before every single client.
>
> Inconsistency in setup is invisible to you and obvious to your client — they notice when the towel warmer is cold or the cart's in the wrong place, even if they can't say why the experience felt off. The order isn't arbitrary — it follows a build sequence: sanitation and structural setup first, then product and tool staging, then comfort, then ambiance, so nothing gets assembled while the room's being sanitized or the client's already in it.
>
> Halo flush — Barbicide rinse, then plain water until clear. Product dishes — three bowls, shampoo, mask, exfoliant, applicator brush alongside. Towel warmer loaded — confirm warm to the touch before the client arrives. Fresh bed linens. Bed warmer on, set appropriately. Room temperature set lower than normal, since the client will be wet. Spa wrap, robe, and slipper socks folded and ready at the station entry. A towel wrapped around the halo nozzle hose — quiets it, prevents spray, maintains atmosphere. Massage oil and lotion ready on the cart. And ambient — music, lighting, diffuser — running before the client enters the room.

### M7-05 — 7.4 Client positioning
**SOURCE:** all 3 positioning checks + safety watch-for/what-to-do + pressure test. **VOICE:** Reference for the 3 checks and the safety guidance.

> Section 7.4 — Client positioning. Three things to confirm before the water runs.
>
> Positioning errors are hard to correct mid-service without breaking the experience — get these right before you start. It takes thirty seconds and prevents a disruption you can't undo.
>
> Halo alignment: client centered under the halo so water hits evenly. Adjust the client's position, not the halo. Shoulder position: top of shoulders one to two inches off the edge of the bed, for clean neck access without sliding. And occipital support: the occipital bone rests in the deepest curve of the headrest, neck naturally relaxed — not extended, not flexed. If the chin is lifting, reposition.
>
> [firmly] A relaxed, neutral neck position isn't just comfort — sustained hyperextension against a hard basin edge is a documented, if rare, safety concern. Keeping the neck supported is the setup detail most directly within your control to reduce that risk, not a guarantee against it.
>
> Watch for: the client reports neck strain, dizziness, or discomfort at any point, or the chin is visibly lifting. Some signals are only about comfort — but if a client ever reports dizziness, visual changes, or slurred speech, stop immediately and treat it as a medical concern, not a positioning fix. What to do: stop the service, adjust the issue, communicate what you're doing and check in, then resume only once she confirms she's comfortable. Never adjust silently or push through to a "natural" stopping point.
>
> [slowly] Cover the client first, then slide the knee bolster into place. Weighted blanket across the chest if using. Eye mask on. Then begin. Doing it out of sequence makes the client feel arranged rather than cared for.
>
> Pressure test your setup: if something unexpected happens, can you adjust without breaking flow? Can you reach everything without stepping away? Does it still work when you're not thinking perfectly? If not, your setup isn't ready.

### M7-06 — Signature interaction: Find the setup mistakes
**SOURCE:** all 4 cards' prompts and reveals. **VOICE:** Reference for all 4.

> Signature interaction: find the setup mistakes. Not every difference is a mistake — for each setup on screen, decide whether something actually needs to change, or whether it's simply a different, functional way of working.
>
> Setup one: the cart's positioned on the practitioner's left. Different doesn't automatically mean wrong — the cart can sit on either side as long as active-use items stay comfortably reachable. Reachability matters; left versus right doesn't.
>
> Setup two: items used throughout the service are positioned well outside the immediate working area. This one needs correction — frequently used items belong within comfortable reach. Repeatedly stepping away interrupts flow and signals the station needs reorganizing.
>
> Setup three: the treatment bed has no optional armrests. Not necessarily a problem — equipment gets judged by function, support, and usability, not by whether it includes every optional feature. Preference isn't the same as a functional requirement.
>
> And setup four: look at the client's head, neck, and shoulder position. This needs correction — positioned too far from the head end, occipital poorly supported, neck extended. Reposition before continuing.
>
> [firmly] Professional setup is judged by function — support, reach, comfort, service flow — not by whether every treatment room looks identical.
