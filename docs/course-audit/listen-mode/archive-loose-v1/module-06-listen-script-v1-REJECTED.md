## The script

### M6-01 — Module Briefing (spoken)
**VOICE:** Teaching.

> Welcome to Module 6. By this point you know how to observe the scalp and recognize scalp types. Now you're adding another layer — conditions that change how those patterns behave. Because if you misread what you're seeing, you'll choose the wrong approach with confidence.
>
> You'll learn what you can and can't conclude from appearance alone, the most important distinction in scalp condition care, how a single spectrum runs from Malassezia to seborrheic dermatitis, exactly when to pause a service or refer out, and what actually makes a condition worse — so you never do it.
>
> [warmly] Pay attention to this: the most dangerous mistake here isn't missing a condition. It's treating a guess like a diagnosis.

### M6-02 — 6.1 Your role here + 6.2 What you can and cannot conclude
**VOICE:** Teaching.

> Section 6.1 — Your role here. Recognize. Understand. Adjust. Know when to stop.
>
> Most clients aren't intentionally mismanaging their scalp — they're guessing. They see flakes and assume dryness. They feel itching and assume dandruff. They see oil and assume they need stronger cleansing. Then they treat based on symptoms instead of cause. Your job isn't to agree with their diagnosis — it's to interpret. You'll regularly see flaking, oil imbalance, irritation, buildup, itching, discomfort. Some fall well within scope. Others require referral. Recognize what you're seeing, understand what may be driving it, adjust your service, and know when not to proceed.
>
> Section 6.2 — What you can and cannot conclude from appearance alone. Appearance is suggestive. It is not conclusive.
>
> Flake color, size, oiliness, and redness tell you something — not everything. Several presentations can look similar at a glance: dry scalp, early dandruff, ordinary product buildup, and, at the more involved end, presentations that resemble other conditions entirely, like psoriasis, tinea capitis, or contact or atopic dermatitis. A cosmetic assessment can't rule those look-alikes out. This module doesn't teach you to name or rule out those other conditions — it teaches you that they exist, so a confident-sounding visual read never becomes more certain than it actually is.
>
> [firmly] When a presentation is ambiguous or mixed, the responsible move is to ask about history — product use, wash timing, recent changes, symptoms — and favor the gentler service direction. Don't force a label just to make the picture feel resolved.

### M6-03 — 6.3 The most important distinction in scalp care
**VOICE:** Reference for both comparison profiles.

> Section 6.3 — The most important distinction in scalp care. Dry scalp and dandruff are not the same thing. They can both present with flaking, but the cause — and the treatment approach — is completely different. Getting this wrong is one of the most common and consequential mistakes in scalp care. There's a side-by-side illustration on screen comparing the two patterns.
>
> Dry scalp is a hydration issue: small, white, powdery flakes, a matte, non-oily surface, tightness or mild itch, minimal visible oil. It flakes because it lacks moisture — the hydrolipid film is depleted, a barrier issue, not a disease process. Causes include aging, seasonal changes, over-cleansing, harsh products, chemical processing. The direction: gentle exfoliation to lift dead cells, then hydration and barrier repair — restore what's missing, don't suppress a biological process.
>
> Dandruff is a biological imbalance: larger, yellowish flakes, oily or clumped near the root, visible oil at the scalp, irritation or redness. It's generally associated with the scalp environment shifting out of balance — excess oil, Malassezia yeast activity, inflammation, and faster skin-cell turnover typically combining, though the exact balance varies by client. This is not a hydration problem — adding moisture to an oily, inflamed scalp can make it worse. The direction: cleansing support, oil control, anti-fungal-supporting ingredients, anti-inflammatory botanicals — rebalance the environment, don't repair a barrier.
>
> [slowly] Some clients show features of both. When that happens, don't force a label — ask about recent products, wash frequency, how long the pattern's been present, and favor the gentler direction until the picture is clearer.

### M6-04 — 6.4 The cycle worth understanding
**SOURCE:** all 6 cycle steps + 3 scenario cards + final reasoning question. **VOICE:** Reference for the cycle and scenarios.

> Section 6.4 — The cycle worth understanding. Why the wrong product makes things worse. This is a common, avoidable pattern — a client sees flakes, assumes dandruff, and reaches for the strongest anti-dandruff product they can find. Here's what actually happens, in order.
>
> One: the client notices flaking on their clothing and scalp, and immediately interprets it as dandruff — almost always without any real assessment. Two: they assume it must be dandruff and buy anti-dandruff shampoo — the logical-seeming, heavily marketed solution. Three: the anti-dandruff shampoo strips the scalp of its remaining moisture and oils — it strips oil, suppresses yeast, and slows cell turnover, removing what little barrier a dry scalp had left. Four: the scalp gets drier, flaking worsens, more irritation follows — barrier loss makes it drier, tighter, more reactive, and the client reads this as the condition getting worse. Five: they increase usage, thinking they need more of the same product — the logical response to something not working. And six: back to step one — the cycle continues, and they arrive at your table having over-treated for months. Your first job is often to stop the damage, not start a new treatment.
>
> [warmly] This is not treatment failure — it's misidentification. Flaking alone doesn't justify anti-dandruff treatment. Your job is to identify what type of flaking you're seeing before recommending anything. Sometimes the best thing you can do is simplify, not add more.
>
> What this looks like in real time, three scenarios. One: fine white flakes, no visible oil, tight matte scalp — a dry-scalp pattern. This doesn't point you toward a stronger dandruff response; treating an already-dry presentation as oil-driven can intensify dryness and reactivity. Support the barrier, reduce stripping, simplify the routine.
>
> Two: yellowish flakes, oil at the root, slight redness — a dandruff-spectrum pattern, supporting a cleansing-and-regulation direction rather than hydration-first. Support cleansing, regulation, and an appropriate within-scope home-care direction.
>
> Three: flakes, some oil, mild irritation, not clean-cut — a mixed or unclear presentation. This is where practitioners overcorrect. The priority isn't the strongest response — it's avoiding an aggressive one before the picture is clearer. Favor balance, avoid aggressive stripping, ask more questions.
>
> Final reasoning: a client arrives after repeatedly escalating anti-dandruff products for a dry, increasingly reactive scalp. What should change first? On screen, you'll choose between moving to a stronger product, reassessing the presentation before choosing a direction, or adding more exfoliation. [firmly] The presentation always gets reassessed before the product direction changes — escalating blind is what created the cycle in the first place.

### M6-06 — Post-pass: 6.5 Malassezia to seborrheic dermatitis + 6.6 When to pause or refer
**SOURCE:** the spectrum + all 5 referral criteria + verbatim script. **VOICE:** Reference for the spectrum and referral list; the referral script verbatim. `gateType: 'post-pass'`, `resumeAfterPass: true`.

> Section 6.5 — Malassezia to seborrheic dermatitis: one spectrum. Not two conditions — one spectrum.
>
> Malassezia is a naturally occurring yeast found on most scalps. It becomes a problem when several factors line up — increased oil, a shift in the scalp's surface environment, and individual variation in how the scalp's immune and inflammatory response reacts to the yeast's activity. As it progresses, it moves along a spectrum — from balanced, to mild dandruff, to moderate, to seborrheic dermatitis. The same underlying process, at different levels of severity. There's an interactive slider on screen if you want to move through that spectrum yourself.
>
> Section 6.6 — When to pause or refer. Know these without needing to touch anything.
>
> Refer when you see: redness or scaling that spreads beyond the scalp — to the eyebrows, hairline, or ears — combined with active irritation. Broken, weeping, crusted, or bleeding skin. Symptoms the client describes as severe, painful, or rapidly worsening. No improvement despite reasonable home care and product changes. Or any presentation you're not confident matches a typical dry-scalp or dandruff-spectrum pattern, including anything that could resemble a different condition entirely.
>
> [slowly] Here's the referral script: "I'm seeing something today that goes beyond what a head spa service is designed to address. I don't want to guess at the cause or risk making it worse, so I'd recommend having it evaluated before we continue with scalp services."

### M6-07 — Signature interaction: Sort three presentations
**VOICE:** Teaching, framing only — choices left to the student.

> Signature interaction: sort three presentations. For each one, decide whether to proceed as usual, proceed with a modification, or pause and refer — weigh the combination of cues together, not any single feature alone.
>
> Presentation one: fine white powdery flakes, matte surface, minimal oil, mild tightness, no redness. Presentation two: yellowish, clumped flakes, visible oil at the root, mild redness, contained to the scalp, no spread. And presentation three: scale and redness spreading to the eyebrows and hairline, significant reported discomfort, persisting for months despite drugstore treatments.
>
> [firmly] Sort all three on screen — proceed, modify, or refer — using everything you just learned about the dry-versus-dandruff distinction and the referral criteria.

### M6-08 — 6.7 Treatment within scope
**SOURCE:** all 4 treatment cards + "product category is not a diagnosis" note. **VOICE:** Reference for all 4.

> Section 6.7 — Treatment within scope. You're supporting the environment. Not treating the condition.
>
> Cleansing support: regular cleansing reduces excess oil, buildup, and the microbial overgrowth Malassezia feeds on. Consistency matters more than intensity.
>
> Anti-fungal supporting ingredients: zinc pyrithione, 1%-strength ketoconazole, and 1% selenium sulfide are OTC categories that help manage yeast activity. Never recommend or name a prescription-strength, 2%, product.
>
> Anti-inflammatory botanicals: licorice root and chamomile calm the scalp, reducing redness, irritation, and discomfort without disrupting the barrier — preferred over aggressive treatments in a head spa context.
>
> And regular maintenance: irregular care leads to fluctuating symptoms. The most effective approach is consistent support over time, not aggressive intervention when things flare.
>
> [warmly] A product category is not a diagnosis. Recommending one is a retail suggestion within a cosmetic service — never a diagnosis, never a prescription. If a presentation meets any of the referral criteria, refer instead of recommending a product.

### M6-09 — 6.8 What makes it worse + Checkpoint 2 (`m6cp2`)
**SOURCE:** all 4 trigger cards + checkpoint prompt. **VOICE:** Reference for all 4 triggers.

> Section 6.8 — What makes it worse. Understanding what aggravates these conditions helps you educate clients in a way that actually changes their behavior.
>
> Stress and hormonal changes: associated with flare-ups, most likely through a combination of immune and inflammatory response and changes in oil production — not oil production alone. Seasonal or cyclical worsening is often a hormonal pattern. Worth asking about at intake, not a diagnosis.
>
> Diet high in sugar, fried, or inflammatory foods: may contribute for some clients, though the evidence is largely individual. Worth asking about — not something to lecture the client on.
>
> Excess oil production: creates a favorable environment for Malassezia. Warmer weather, humidity, and hormonal shifts are commonly associated with more oil between washes, though the relationship isn't precise enough to apply as a fixed rule.
>
> And wrong product use: the most correctable trigger. Overly harsh or stripping products worsen both dryness and dandruff. Clients often arrive already over-treating — simplification is often more effective than adding more.
>
> [firmly] This layers on top of Module 5's scalp-type analysis, not replaces it. Is this dry? Oil-driven? Microbial? Mixed? And then — what matters most right now? The practitioner who can answer those quickly and correctly is the one making decisions instead of guesses.
>
> Here's your final checkpoint. Same client, but this time the microscope shows yellowish clumped flakes near the follicle, visible oiliness, mild redness, and the client reports it spreads to her eyebrows and hairline. How does your response change — and is this still within the scope of a head spa service?
>
> Take your time, and answer above.
