## The script

### M5-01 — Module Briefing (spoken)
**VOICE:** Teaching.

> Welcome to Module 5. Module 4 taught you how to gather and describe evidence. Now you'll turn that evidence — plus what the client reports and how each region responds — into an actual cosmetic service plan. The goal isn't one permanent label for the scalp. It's making each step earn its place.
>
> You'll turn evidence into a real service plan, learn what you can realistically change in a single appointment, set priority order when more than one concern shows up, adapt one protocol to more than one client in real time, and recognize the errors that make a protocol less intelligent, not more.
>
> [warmly] Pay attention to this: a scalp pattern is a starting point, not a permanent label. The plan should adapt as the evidence does.

### M5-02 — 5.1 From evidence to action + 5.2 What you can actually change
**VOICE:** Teaching, Reference for both lists.

> Section 5.1 — From evidence to action. The scalp doesn't need a label. The service needs a reason.
>
> A useful assessment changes what you do next — how thoroughly you cleanse, whether you exfoliate, how much heat you use, where you place product, how much pressure you apply, or whether you pause entirely. The weak approach notices one feature, assigns one type, and runs the same protocol everywhere. The stronger approach combines what's visible with what the client reports, what happened recently, and how each region responds. Two clients with different presentations don't need the same service — and neither can be reduced to one word.
>
> Ask four questions before you choose a step: what's visibly present? What does the client report? What's the safest service limit? And what should change in this region? [firmly] Limit first. Priority second. Region by region.
>
> Section 5.2 — What you can actually change. Most protocol changes come from five levers, and skilled customization is usually a deliberate change in intensity, placement, duration, or omission — not a completely different service.
>
> Cleansing: how thorough, whether one region needs more attention, whether repeating a cleanse is appropriate. More lather isn't the goal — a clean, comfortable finish is.
>
> Exfoliation: whether it belongs at all, and if so, region, method, intensity, and contact time. Visible scale is not automatic permission to exfoliate.
>
> Water and steam: temperature, duration, distance. Heat helps some clients and bothers others — steam is optional, not proof the service is more advanced.
>
> Pressure and tempo: adjusted to comfort and region. Pressure is never a corrective tool — firm massage should never overpower tenderness, burning, or a reactive presentation.
>
> And product placement and finish: where product belongs, how much, and how the finish should feel. Whole-scalp application isn't automatically more thorough.
>
> [slowly] Product comes after the decision. Don't choose the protocol because you want to use a particular product — choose the service direction first, then select a compatible product within your license, training, and manufacturer instructions.

### M5-03 — 5.3 Priority order
**VOICE:** Reference for all 5 items.

> Section 5.3 — Priority order. The safest limit outranks the client's request. Clients may ask for the deepest cleanse or the firmest pressure — their preference matters, but it doesn't override what the assessment tells you is appropriate today.
>
> One, safety limit: is there any reason to avoid the area, pause, or refer? Broken, bleeding, weeping, oozing, draining, pustular, severely painful, or rapidly worsening findings don't need a stronger cosmetic protocol.
>
> Two, client comfort and reactivity: stinging, burning, tenderness, itching, heat, tightness, recent chemical services, scratching, friction, or a recent product reaction.
>
> Three, surface tolerance: whether cleansing, exfoliation, heat, or friction is likely to make the current presentation less comfortable.
>
> Four, visible cosmetic need: what oil, residue, loose scale, or regional difference can be addressed conservatively.
>
> And five, client preference and maintenance goal — once the limits are clear, shape the experience around comfort and what the client actually wants.
>
> [warmly] The strongest service isn't the one with the most steps. It's the one where every step has a reason, and nothing gets added just to prove value.

### M5-04 — 5.4 Patterns, not permanent types
**VOICE:** Reference for all 5 patterns.

> Section 5.4 — Patterns, not permanent types. Read the dominant need without forcing the whole scalp into it.
>
> Baseline or maintenance: no dominant concern, no meaningful regional difference, no discomfort — after checking wash timing and any recent chemical service, preserve. Gentle, non-disruptive cleanse, no unnecessary exfoliation, focus on comfort. Don't conclude the scalp has one universal "healthy" color, or that every corrective step should be added just because the client paid for a premium service.
>
> Oil-dominant or residue-present: diffuse shine, slick roots, visible coating — after checking wash timing, dry shampoo, and whether it changes after cleansing, cleanse thoroughly but proportionately, with targeted placement and full rinse. Don't conclude the follicles are clogged or that cleansing will trigger compensatory oil production.
>
> Fine-scale or dry-appearing: matte surface, fine loose scale, reported tightness — after checking itching, burning, and cleansing frequency, lower unnecessary cleansing intensity, use comfortable water temperature, reduce friction. Don't conclude the image proves dehydration, barrier damage, or dermatitis.
>
> Mixed regional: shine in one region, scale or sensitivity in another — map the regions, and cleanse, exfoliate, condition, or reduce stimulation only where each decision is earned. Don't let one dominant feature control the protocol across the entire scalp.
>
> And reactive presentation or reported sensitivity: visible difference from baseline plus stinging, burning, tenderness, or a recent reaction — reduce stimulation, lower heat, avoid aggressive exfoliation and firm massage, pause or refer when the presentation falls outside cosmetic limits. Don't conclude visible color alone proves sensitivity, inflammation, or infection.
>
> [slowly] There's a case-study photo on screen showing one real scalp with two different regional needs side by side — the crown calling for more targeted cleansing, the hairline calling for a gentler approach. Same client, two different decisions.

### M5-05 — Signature interaction: What changes first?
**SOURCE:** all 4 scenarios (framing + options narrated, correct-direction reasoning given, choice left to student). **VOICE:** Teaching.

> Signature interaction: what changes first? You're not diagnosing the scalp — you're deciding what the current evidence allows you to do next. Four scenarios, on screen, each with three possible directions to choose from.
>
> Scenario one: no dominant concern, no regional difference, no discomfort, client wants relaxation. Scenario two: the crown shows diffuse shine and residue, the sides and hairline look close to baseline, no burning or tenderness, and the client used dry shampoo that morning. Scenario three: the hairline shows fine loose scale, and the client reports burning after starting a new product three days ago. And scenario four: one assessment area is broken, moist, and visibly draining.
>
> [firmly] Across all four, the same logic applies: preserve what doesn't need correcting, target the specific evidence you actually have, reduce stimulation when there's reported discomfort, and stop contact entirely — pause and refer — the moment skin integrity is in question. Work through all four on screen before moving on.

### M5-07 — Post-pass: 5.5 One appointment, more than one approach
**VOICE:** Reference for the 5-category framework and all 3 client scripts referenced later; here Teaching + the preserve/modify/avoid/pause/refer list. `gateType: 'post-pass'`, `resumeAfterPass: true`.

> Section 5.5 — One appointment, more than one approach. The protocol can change without the service feeling fragmented. Use the same structure, then adjust the levers by area — cleanse the crown more thoroughly, skip exfoliation near a reactive hairline, lower pressure at the temples, use a lighter finish at the roots. The client still experiences one cohesive service. You're just no longer pretending every square inch has the same need.
>
> Five categories: preserve — keep what's already appropriate, don't remove a comfortable, effective part of the service just because another area needs modification. Modify — change intensity, placement, duration, temperature, pressure, or product amount. Avoid — omit a step that's unnecessary, incompatible, or likely to increase discomfort. Pause — stop when new symptoms, visible changes, contamination, or discomfort make continuing inappropriate. And refer — recommend medical evaluation when the presentation is outside cosmetic limits.
>
> [slowly] Document the regional decision in plain language: what you observed, what the client reported, what you changed. Never document a diagnosis you're not qualified to make.

### M5-08 — 5.6 Choose a function, not a fantasy + 5.7 Intensity is part of the protocol
**VOICE:** Reference for both 5- and 4-item lists.

> Section 5.6 — Choose a function, not a fantasy. Product knowledge matters, but product enthusiasm can distort judgment. A practitioner who starts with the bottle often finds a reason to use it — start with the service need instead.
>
> Gentle routine cleansing, for maintenance and comfort. Targeted or more thorough cleansing, when visible oil or residue and client history support it. Optional compatible exfoliation, only when the skin is intact, the client is comfortable, and the product directions support it. Conditioning or hydration support, for comfort and softness — never described as treating a medical barrier disorder. And a minimal-product service, when the client reports recent reactivity or product history is unclear.
>
> [firmly] Specific ingredients, medicated claims, and condition-directed products may carry license, labeling, contraindication, or manufacturer requirements. Follow the product — not a social-media protocol.
>
> Section 5.7 — Intensity is part of the protocol. More heat, more pressure, and more time don't equal more expertise.
>
> Steam may support comfort, but it's optional — reduce or omit it for heat sensitivity or a recent reaction, and never claim it opens pores or detoxifies. Water temperature: comfortably lukewarm as a default, cooler when heat increases discomfort. Pressure: controlled and responsive to feedback, never a corrective response to oil, scale, or residue. And time: longer contact isn't automatically better — use the shortest effective step that supports the goal.
>
> [slowly] The protocol isn't the number of steps. It's the quality of the decisions between them.

### M5-09 — 5.8 Client communication
**SOURCE:** all 3 scripts, verbatim. **VOICE:** Reference for all 3 quoted scripts.

> Section 5.8 — Client communication. Redirect the method without dismissing the goal. Clients often ask for intensity because they associate intensity with results. Your job isn't to argue with the goal — it's to explain why a different method is the better fit today.
>
> Here's the mixed-regional script: "Your scalp is not presenting the same way in every area today. I'm going to cleanse the crown more thoroughly, keep the hairline gentler, and adjust the product placement so we do not overcorrect the areas that do not need it."
>
> The lower-intensity script: "You asked for the deepest version of the service. Based on what you're feeling and what I'm observing, the stronger exfoliation, maximum steam, and firm pressure are not the best fit today. I can still give you a complete service, but I'm going to lower the heat, reduce stimulation, and focus on a gentle cleanse and comfort."
>
> And the pause-and-refer script: "I'm seeing an area that is not appropriate for cosmetic treatment today. I do not want to aggravate it or guess at the cause, so I recommend having it evaluated before we continue with scalp services."
>
> [warmly] Client satisfaction isn't created by saying yes to every requested step. It's created by giving the client a clear reason, a thoughtful alternative when one is appropriate, and a service plan you can defend.

### M5-10 — 5.9 Errors that make protocols less intelligent + Checkpoint 2 (`m5cp2`)
**SOURCE:** all 8 error patterns + checkpoint prompt. **VOICE:** Reference for all 8.

> Section 5.9 — Errors that make protocols less intelligent. The most common mistake is treating the label instead of the client.
>
> Label first — choosing "oily," "dry," or "sensitive" before gathering regional evidence. Better: gather the evidence and context first.
>
> Product first — building the explanation around the product you want to use. Better: define the need first, then choose a compatible product.
>
> Whole scalp, one intensity — applying the strongest regional need everywhere. Better: map the regions and adjust only where the evidence supports it.
>
> Exfoliating every flake — treating visible scale as proof exfoliation is required. Better: check comfort, skin integrity, and history first.
>
> Strongest means best — assuming maximum steam or pressure creates more value. Better: use the level the goal actually requires, not the maximum available.
>
> Image becomes diagnosis — turning shine, scale, or color change into a medical conclusion. Better: describe what's visible, ask for context, stay inside cosmetic observation.
>
> Client request becomes permission — continuing an inappropriate step because the client asked. Better: explain the safer modification, or pause the service.
>
> And improvement becomes cure — describing a cleaner appearance as proof a condition was treated. Better: describe the cosmetic change or comfort, without claiming a cure.
>
> [firmly] A strong protocol is explainable — you should be able to name what you observed, what changed your interpretation, what you modified, and what you intentionally left out.
>
> Here's your final checkpoint. A client reports stinging and tenderness, and you observe a reactive-appearing area. They still ask for the strongest exfoliation, maximum steam, and firm massage. What would you say, how would you modify or pause the service, and what would make you refer instead of proceeding?
>
> Take your time, and answer above.
