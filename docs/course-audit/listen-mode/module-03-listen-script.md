## The script

### M3-01 — Module Briefing (spoken)
**SOURCE:** `.mod-opener`. **VOICE:** Teaching.

> Welcome to Module 3. This is where the course turns technical — but the goal isn't to memorize anatomy for its own sake. Once you understand the structures and cycles beneath the scalp, buildup, shedding, sensitivity, and product response stop looking random.
>
> You'll map the scalp and hair structures you're actually working with, understand the hair-growth cycle and what disrupts it, and learn how barrier function and surface lipids shape what you see. You'll connect all of it to the massage techniques you'll actually use — and turn anatomy into action, not just terminology you memorize.
>
> [warmly] Pay attention to this: once you can explain why a scalp behaves the way it does, service adaptation stops being guesswork.

### M3-02 — 3.1 Read beneath the surface
**SOURCE:** Section 3.1 + Cadence note. **VOICE:** Teaching, Reference for the quoted Cadence note.

> Section 3.1 — Read beneath the surface. The scalp is not a backdrop. It's the environment everything depends on.
>
> Every product, tool, and hand movement meets a living skin environment before it affects the hair. The scalp follows the same fundamental rules as skin elsewhere on the body, but it contains a dense concentration of follicles, sebaceous glands, nerves, and blood vessels.
>
> That changes how an experienced practitioner thinks. Instead of reacting to one visible clue, you start connecting surface appearance with product history, barrier condition, sebum, sensitivity, and hair-cycle timing. Anatomy isn't a list of terms to memorize — it's the map behind better decisions.
>
> [slowly] The advantage isn't knowing the longest anatomical word. It's knowing which structure makes a visible clue matter — and which conclusions the surface can't support.

### M3-03 — 3.2 The scalp map
**SOURCE:** Section 3.2, all 5 SCALP-acronym layers. **VOICE:** Teaching for framing, Reference for all 5 layers in order.

> Section 3.2 — The scalp map. Five layers, one surface you can actually touch.
>
> The scalp is commonly described in five layers — the acronym SCALP makes the order easy to remember. Your service takes place at the surface; the deeper layers explain the tissue beneath your hands, not five separate targets to treat.
>
> S — Skin. The epidermis and dermis contain the surface barrier, follicles, sebaceous glands, sensory receptors, and many of the visible clues you use during cosmetic assessment. This is where product choice, cleansing, temperature, exfoliation, and tolerance actually get judged.
>
> C — Dense connective tissue. A firm, fibrous layer containing many of the scalp's vessels and nerves. It helps explain why the scalp is highly vascular and sensitive — it's not a layer a cosmetic service selectively targets.
>
> A — Galea aponeurotica. A broad fibrous sheet connecting the frontal and occipital muscles. It contributes to how the scalp moves as a tissue unit. Routine massage doesn't isolate or "treat" the galea.
>
> L — Loose areolar tissue. A deeper glide plane that lets the upper scalp layers move over the skull — anatomical context, not a cosmetic treatment target.
>
> And P — Pericranium, the membrane covering the outer surface of the skull. It's not reached or treated during a head spa service at all.
>
> [firmly] Anatomy makes pressure more intelligent. It does not turn deeper tissue into a cosmetic claim.

### M3-04 — 3.3 The living system beneath the strand + Anatomy to action
**SOURCE:** Section 3.3 + diagram reference + all 4 "Anatomy to action" structures. **VOICE:** Teaching for framing, Reference for all 4 structures (each with observe/changes/don't-assume).

> Section 3.3 — The living system beneath the strand. The strand is the output. The follicle is the living system.
>
> The visible hair shaft is made of keratinized, nonliving cells. It can be cleansed, conditioned, protected, and cosmetically damaged — but it doesn't heal itself. Beneath the surface, the follicle is a living mini-organ: the lower follicle contains the bulb and surrounds the dermal papilla, the sebaceous gland releases sebum into the upper follicular canal, and the arrector pili muscle attaches to the follicular unit. This is why strand care and scalp care are related but not interchangeable — and why a surface service can't diagnose or directly treat internal follicle function.
>
> [slowly] There's a diagram on screen mapping out this whole cross-section — the hair shaft, the layers, the follicle and bulb, the vessels and nerves. Worth a look if you're following along visually, but everything on it is covered here too.
>
> Now — anatomy to action. Four structures, each with what you can actually observe, what it changes in the service, and just as important, what it doesn't let you assume.
>
> Surface barrier, the stratum corneum: the scalp's principal protective and permeability barrier. You may observe dryness, visible scale, oil, redness, product residue, or altered sheen. It changes your cleanser strength, exfoliation choice, water temperature, product load, and whether to simplify the service. What not to assume: one surface clue doesn't establish dandruff, dehydration, infection, inflammation, or the cause of sensitivity.
>
> Follicle opening and sebaceous gland: part of the pilosebaceous unit, releasing sebum into the upper follicular canal. You may observe material around follicular openings, surface oil, scale, or cosmetic residue. It changes how carefully you cleanse, section, rinse, and whether exfoliation is appropriate. What not to assume: visible material doesn't prove a follicle is "clogged," or identify the cause.
>
> Follicle, bulb, and dermal papilla: the living system beneath the strand. You cannot see the bulb or dermal papilla during a routine surface assessment. This keeps hair-growth language honest — cosmetic care can support cleanliness and comfort at the surface; it does not directly feed, repair, or restart the dermal papilla. What not to assume: surface appearance can't establish why a follicle is shedding, miniaturizing, inflamed, or no longer producing hair.
>
> And vessels and nerves: the scalp's vascular and sensory supply. What you observe here is the client's response — to pressure, temperature, tenderness, touch. It changes your pressure, pace, positioning, temperature, and whether to avoid an irritated or painful area. What not to assume: temporary warmth or redness doesn't prove nutrients reached the follicles, or that growth was stimulated.
>
> [firmly] The expert move isn't naming more structures. It's knowing what each structure actually allows you to conclude.

### M3-05 — 3.4 The hair-growth cycle + Predict before you reveal
**SOURCE:** Section 3.4, all 4 phases + the timing-prediction interaction (framing only, choice left to the student). **VOICE:** Teaching for framing, Reference for all 4 phases in order.

> Section 3.4 — The hair-growth cycle. Hair loss is often a timing problem before it's a pattern problem.
>
> Every follicle cycles independently through growth, transition, rest, and release. That staggered timing is why normal shedding is continuous — and why a major event may not show up in the hair until months later.
>
> Anagen, growth: the follicle actively produces the hair shaft. Scalp anagen lasts years, which is why scalp hair can grow long. The length of anagen varies — a surface service can't extend it on command.
>
> Catagen, transition: the lower follicle regresses over a period of weeks, and the growing phase ends. Catagen is brief, and represents transition, not sudden surface damage.
>
> Telogen, relative rest: the follicle stays in a resting phase for roughly several months before the retained club hair is released. A trigger that shifts more follicles into telogen may not produce visible shedding right away.
>
> And exogen, release — often described as the shedding portion of the cycle. The club hair separates and sheds from the follicle. Shedding is a normal part of cycling; the pattern, amount, timing, duration, and accompanying signs are what determine whether it deserves closer evaluation.
>
> [slowly] The event and the shedding may be separated by months. That delay is one of the most useful pieces of history a practitioner can recognize.
>
> Predict before you reveal: a client has a high fever today. When could related shedding become noticeable? On screen you'll see three options — within the same week, about two to three weeks later, or roughly two to three months later. Think through what you just learned about the cycle, and make your prediction before moving on.

### M3-07 — Post-pass: 3.5 Read the pattern
**SOURCE:** Section 3.5, all 3 condition cards including the verbatim referral script. **VOICE:** Teaching for framing, Reference for all 3 cards. `gateType: 'post-pass'`, `resumeAfterPass: true`.

> Section 3.5 — Read the pattern. Don't start with the name. Start with the pattern.
>
> A client may arrive asking for a diagnosis. Your advantage isn't guessing the label faster — it's noticing the timing, distribution, duration, and accompanying signs that determine the next professional step.
>
> Delayed diffuse shedding: increased shedding across the scalp, beginning several weeks to a few months after a major illness, high fever, surgery, medication change, rapid weight change, childbirth, or another significant physiological stressor. The delay matters — a client may dismiss the relevant event because it didn't happen recently. Your move: document the timeline, describe the diffuse pattern, explain that delayed shedding can follow major body changes, and recommend medical evaluation when it's heavy, persistent, worsening, or concerning.
>
> Postpartum shedding: noticeable diffuse shedding beginning in the months after pregnancy, as hair cycling shifts again. It can feel sudden, because more hairs may release within a similar period rather than the usual staggered pattern. Your move: normalize that postpartum shedding is common, without guaranteeing the cause or timeline. Keep the service gentle, and recommend medical evaluation for patchy, painful, severe, persistent, or otherwise concerning loss.
>
> And a pattern requiring medical evaluation: patchy or asymmetric loss, rapidly changing density, smooth or shiny areas, significant redness or scale, pain, burning, bleeding, crusting, pustules, broken skin, or loss involving brows or lashes. Here, the most valuable decision you can make may be not to treat the area. Document what's visible, explain the limit of cosmetic assessment, pause or modify the service when appropriate, and refer to a dermatologist or qualified medical professional.
>
> [slowly] Here's the referral script, word for word: "I'm seeing a pattern that deserves a medical evaluation before we treat this area. I can document what is visible, but I can't determine the cause from a cosmetic assessment."
>
> A commonly quoted daily shedding range is context — not a diagnostic test. The change from the client's baseline, the pattern, and the timeline matter more than counting one day's hairs.

### M3-08 — 3.6 Barrier and surface lipids
**SOURCE:** Section 3.6, the 3-question checklist + Cadence note. **VOICE:** Teaching, Reference for the 3-question checklist and the quoted note.

> Section 3.6 — Barrier and surface lipids. Oil, flakes, and tightness are clues — not conclusions.
>
> The stratum corneum is the scalp's principal protective barrier — it helps limit water loss and reduces penetration by outside irritants. Sebum, sweat, moisture, and other surface components create a thin surface environment, often described in cosmetic education as the hydrolipid film. Sebum contributes lubrication, but the barrier is more than surface oil.
>
> This distinction matters because a scalp can look oily and still be irritated, or look flaky for reasons that have nothing to do with inadequate cleansing. Harsh or overly frequent cleansing may contribute to dryness and barrier disruption — it doesn't prove the sebaceous glands will automatically produce more oil in response.
>
> Before you reach for a stronger cleanser, ask three questions. One: what does the scalp feel like — comfortable, tight, itchy, tender, or burning? Two: what's been used on it — clarifying shampoo, medicated products, dry shampoo, chemical services, oils, or frequent exfoliation? And three: what else is visible — oil, redness, adherent scale, loose flakes, residue, or broken skin?
>
> [warmly] A newer practitioner sees one symptom and chooses one product. A stronger practitioner reads the combination. Flaking plus tightness plus frequent clarifying tells a different story than flaking plus heavy oil and adherent scale.
>
> Treat the pattern you can support cosmetically — not the diagnosis you cannot establish.

### M3-09 — 3.7 Massage and anatomy
**SOURCE:** Section 3.7, the "what anatomy changes in your hands" list. **VOICE:** Teaching, Reference for the list.

> Section 3.7 — Massage and anatomy. Massage is technique, not mythology.
>
> Scalp massage is valuable because of how it feels and how well it can be controlled — pressure, rhythm, pace, tissue movement, warmth, and the client's response. Manual stimulation may temporarily increase local skin blood flow. Some limited small studies have explored possible changes in hair thickness, but that evidence doesn't support promising hair growth, nutrient delivery, follicle activation, or treatment of hair-loss conditions.
>
> The experienced practitioner doesn't make massage sound more medical to make it sound more valuable. The value is in precise, comfortable, repeatable technique.
>
> What anatomy actually changes in your hands: use pressure the client can comfortably receive. Adjust for tenderness, inflammation, broken skin, recent procedures, and sensitivity. Don't assume harder pressure reaches a deeper or more beneficial target. Don't massage an area that should be medically evaluated first. And keep the purpose clear — controlled manual care, relaxation, comfort, and a cohesive service experience.
>
> [slowly] The strongest explanation is also the cleanest: massage is a skilled part of the experience. You don't need a hair-growth promise to justify doing it well.

### M3-10 — 3.8 Anatomy in practice + Checkpoint 2 (`cp2`)
**SOURCE:** Section 3.8, the 4 background questions + checkpoint prompt. **VOICE:** Teaching, Reference for the 4-question list.

> Section 3.8 — Anatomy in practice. Anatomy should disappear into judgment.
>
> During a service, you won't silently name every layer or phase. The knowledge should run in the background. You see flakes and ask what else is present before choosing a cleanser. You hear "my hair started shedding" and ask what happened two or three months earlier. You see patchy loss or compromised skin and know the correct next step may happen outside the treatment room. That's internalization — the science becomes a faster, calmer decision.
>
> Four questions running in the background: one, what am I actually seeing or hearing? Two, which structure, cycle, or barrier function helps explain it? Three, what can a cosmetic service responsibly change? And four, what requires a different professional, or a different day?
>
> [firmly] You're not collecting anatomy terms. You're reducing guesswork.
>
> Here's your second checkpoint for this module. A client has scalp tightness and visible flaking after using a strong clarifying shampoo every day. Explain what the scalp barrier and surface lipid film do, what may have disrupted them, and one conservative change you'd make to the service.
>
> Take your time, and answer above.
