# AIMT Module 12 — Final Practitioner Conversation Bank

**Status:** LOCKED student-facing wording authority for implementation  
**Course:** AIMT Head Spa Certification Course  
**Bank size:** 9 conversations  
**Student exam draw:** 3 conversations per attempt  
**Authority:** This file supersedes raw interview wording in `module-12-final-exam-raw-blueprint.md` for Part III student-facing content.

## Tone and behavior rules

Part III must feel noticeably different from Parts I and II. Cadence should sound like a knowledgeable tutor/counselor having a final professional conversation—not like a proctor reading more test questions.

Use:
- the student’s first name naturally where available;
- contractions and conversational wording;
- one question at a time;
- short natural transitions;
- one targeted follow-up only when needed.

Avoid:
- “According to Module X…”
- repeating the rubric;
- exaggerated praise;
- interrogation;
- asking students to tell stories from work experience they may not have.

### Scoring model

Each selected conversation uses **5 prompt-specific criteria**, each scored:

- **0** — not demonstrated / incorrect
- **1** — partially demonstrated
- **2** — clearly demonstrated

Each conversation therefore has **10 possible points**. Three conversations yield 30 raw points, converted to a Part III percentage.

The single allowed follow-up is **not bonus credit**. It is a chance to clarify one incomplete criterion. Cadence may update that criterion based on the follow-up.

Any critical-domain Type A flag remains separate from the numeric score.

## Natural transition library

After a resolved conversation, Cadence may say:

> That makes sense. I’ve got what I need there. Let’s look at another situation.

or:

> Got it. That gives me a clear picture of how you’re thinking about it. Let’s switch gears.

When one follow-up is required:

> There’s one piece I want to hear a little more about before we move on.

After Conversation 3:

> Thanks, [First Name]. That’s everything I needed from you. I’m submitting this part with the rest of your assessment now.

# INT-01 — Professional Boundaries

**Modules:** 1, 4, 11  
**Evidence:** Critical-Domain Evidence: D1  
**Primary competency:** Observation vs. diagnosis; tool output vs. practitioner authority

## Cadence primary prompt

> Let’s start with something you’ve had to think about throughout the course. Say a client really wants you to tell her what’s going on with her scalp. You’ve looked at it yourself, you have magnified images, and maybe an AI tool is even suggesting a condition. How would you handle that conversation? What information would you use, and where would you draw the line?

## Allowed follow-up, if needed

> Okay. What if she pushes a little and says, “I know you can’t officially diagnose me — just tell me what you think it probably is”? How would you answer that?

## Rubric criteria — 10 points

1. **Uses available information appropriately** — recognizes observation, magnified images, and AI output can inform attention/judgment without becoming diagnostic proof.
2. **Maintains description vs. diagnosis boundary** — clearly distinguishes what can be described from what cannot be confirmed.
3. **Does not use “probably” as a workaround diagnosis** — preserves the boundary even when the client invites an informal conclusion.
4. **Connects findings to a responsible service/referral next step** — does not stop at “I can’t diagnose.”
5. **Recognizes authority source** — does not imply AIMT certification or technology expands legal/professional authority.

**Critical Type A flags:** confirming a named diagnosis as fact; claiming AI/microscopy establishes diagnosis; claiming certification grants diagnostic authority.

**Source:** Module 1 scope; Module 4 observation discipline; Module 11 AI authority/confidence-score framework.

# INT-02 — Consent Can Change

**Modules:** 2, 8  
**Evidence:** Critical-Domain Evidence: D3  
**Primary competency:** Changing consent; bodywork authority

## Cadence primary prompt

> Here’s one that could easily happen in a real service. A client told you at intake that she didn’t want neck or shoulder massage, so you left it out. Halfway through, she changes her mind and asks if you can add it. Assume it’s within your scope and training. What would you do from there?

## Allowed follow-up, if needed

> And what if what she asked for wasn’t actually within your scope or training — does her asking for it change anything?

## Rubric criteria — 10 points

1. **Respects the earlier decline** — recognizes the original “no” was valid and correctly followed.
2. **Recognizes consent may change** — does not treat the earlier decline as permanent if the client changes her mind.
3. **Reconfirms permission intentionally** — does not treat the new request as automatic permission to start touching.
4. **Preserves practical privacy/positioning** — recognizes any added bodywork still needs appropriate positioning/draping/privacy.
5. **Maintains scope/training boundary** — understands client consent cannot create practitioner authority.

**Critical Type A flags:** beginning the added bodywork without intentional consent confirmation; performing work outside applicable scope/training because the client requested it.

**Source:** Module 2 consent/autonomy; Module 8 bodywork scope and changing preferences.

# INT-03 — You’re Running Behind

**Modules:** 7, 10  
**Evidence:** Critical-Domain Evidence: D4  
**Primary competency:** Sanitation/process integrity under schedule pressure

## Cadence primary prompt

> Let’s say it’s one of those days where everything is running a little behind. Your next client is already waiting, but part of your sanitation or equipment process still isn’t finished. What can you adjust to get yourself caught up, and what are you absolutely not willing to rush?

## Allowed follow-up, if needed

> If the room just isn’t ready yet, what would you actually say to the client who’s waiting?

## Rubric criteria — 10 points

1. **Protects required contact/process time** — clearly states required times are not shortened.
2. **Identifies legitimate flexibility** — can name independent tasks/order/alternatives that may flex.
3. **Rejects unprocessed substitutions** — does not use an item/process that is not ready.
4. **Maintains clean/used integrity** — does not allow time pressure to collapse the sanitation system.
5. **Handles client communication professionally** — calmly manages the wait rather than transferring pressure or hiding the issue.

**Critical Type A flags:** shortening required contact/process time; using an unprocessed item; treating visible cleanliness as completed processing.

**Source:** Module 7 prep sequencing; Module 10 Reset Under Pressure.

# INT-04 — The Client Wants More

**Modules:** 5, 8, 9  
**Evidence:** Critical-Domain Evidence: D2  
**Primary competency:** Safety/appropriateness vs. client preference

## Cadence primary prompt

> A client really wants the works — stronger exfoliation, more steam, more intensity. But based on what you’re seeing and what she’s telling you, you don’t think that’s the right service for her today. She understands that and still says she wants it. How do you handle that?

## Allowed follow-up, if needed

> What if she says, “I’ll sign something saying it was my choice”? Would that change your decision?

## Rubric criteria — 10 points

1. **Professional judgment outranks preference** — recognizes client request/consent does not make an inappropriate service appropriate.
2. **Explains the decision without diagnosing** — gives a clear, professional reason tied to today’s presentation/tolerance.
3. **Redirects rather than simply complies or becomes adversarial** — offers a compatible alternative when appropriate.
4. **Knows when to pause/decline** — does not assume every conflict can be solved by “meeting in the middle.”
5. **Rejects waiver logic** — understands paperwork does not transfer professional responsibility.

**Critical Type A flags:** proceeding at an inappropriate intensity because the client agrees/signs; treating consent as permission to override a safety limit.

**Source:** Module 5 decision priority; Module 8 adaptation; Module 9 restraint-over-sale.

# INT-05 — Don’t Let One Image Tell the Whole Story

**Modules:** 4, 5, 6  
**Evidence:** Standard with D1/D2 crossover  
**Primary competency:** Regional assessment; uncertainty; service translation

## Cadence primary prompt

> Imagine the crown looks pretty dramatic under magnification — oil, residue, scale — but the temples look completely different and most of the rest of the scalp is fairly calm. Then the client asks, “So what type of scalp do I have?” How would you explain what you’re seeing and turn that into today’s service plan?

## Allowed follow-up, if needed

> What if the crown looks like it could handle more cleansing, but the hairline is tender and reactive? How would you think about intensity across the whole scalp?

## Rubric criteria — 10 points

1. **Does not let one dramatic image define the whole scalp.**
2. **Uses regional/distribution reasoning** — describes different areas separately.
3. **Avoids permanent scalp-type/diagnostic labeling.**
4. **Translates findings into regional service decisions** — not one uniform correction.
5. **Protects the reactive region without forcing its lower intensity onto unrelated stable regions** — unless the broader facts actually justify a whole-service change.

**Critical Type A flag:** only if the student explicitly ignores a true safety-limiting region and continues an inappropriate intensity there.

**Source:** Module 4 distribution/observation; Module 5 regional adaptation; Module 6 pattern uncertainty.

# INT-06 — The Service Changes Midway Through

**Modules:** 2, 7, 8  
**Evidence:** Standard with D2/D3 support  
**Primary competency:** Service leadership under changing conditions

## Cadence primary prompt

> Services don’t always go exactly the way you planned them. Say you’re running a little long, the client asks you to lighten your pressure, and then she tells you she doesn’t want the fragrance you started with anymore. How would you manage the rest of that appointment without making it feel chaotic?

## Allowed follow-up, if needed

> What parts of the service would you refuse to rush or compress just to get yourself back on schedule?

## Rubric criteria — 10 points

1. **Honors changing client preferences** — pressure/fragrance are adjusted rather than treated as fixed from intake.
2. **Keeps communication concise and calm** — does not over-narrate or silently ignore changes.
3. **Uses pacing judgment** — recovers time only from legitimately flexible portions.
4. **Protects non-negotiables** — safety checks, required product/process directions, consent/comfort, necessary rinse/transition completeness.
5. **Maintains service leadership** — adapts without visible panic, blame, or rushing the client.

**Critical Type A flags:** explicit refusal to honor changed consent/preference around touch; knowingly compressing a required safety/process step to get back on time.

**Source:** Module 2 active autonomy; Module 7 comfort response; Module 8 pacing/communication/preferences.

# INT-07 — Pricing Something You Can Stand Behind

**Modules:** 9  
**Evidence:** Standard  
**Primary competency:** Business pricing judgment

## Cadence primary prompt

> Let’s switch gears and talk business for a minute. You create a new Head Spa service and now you have to put a real price on it. How would you work your way to a number you actually feel good about charging?

## Allowed follow-up, if needed

> Now say your numbers point to about $205, but most nearby businesses are around $175. What would you do with that information?

## Rubric criteria — 10 points

1. **Uses a complete cost base** — direct/variable costs plus allocated overhead.
2. **Includes full practitioner time** — not only hands-on treatment minutes.
3. **Uses a deliberately chosen margin / understands margin vs. markup.**
4. **Treats competitor pricing as context, not the formula.**
5. **Integrates service design/capacity/positioning/market fit before deciding.**

**Source:** Module 9 pricing strategy and checkpoint `m10cp1` (historical internal ID).

# INT-08 — Using AI Well

**Modules:** 11, 1  
**Evidence:** D1-adjacent  
**Primary competency:** AI authority by task; verification

## Cadence primary prompt

> You’ve obviously used AI throughout this course with me, so let’s make this practical. What’s one part of your own practice where you’d be comfortable letting AI do a lot of the first-pass work? And what’s one area where you’d want the final decision to stay with you? Tell me why you see those differently.

## Allowed follow-up, if needed

> Say the AI gives you a really convincing source or citation for something you want to tell a client. What happens before you actually use it?

## Rubric criteria — 10 points

1. **Provides a realistic low-/moderate-authority AI use** — drafting, organizing, brainstorming, etc.
2. **Provides a realistic human-final-authority use** — diagnosis/referral/safety/scope/client-facing professional judgment.
3. **Explains the difference in terms of stakes/authority, not merely “AI can be wrong.”**
4. **Demonstrates verification discipline for factual/research output.**
5. **Maintains human ownership of what is ultimately communicated/used.**

**Critical Type A flag:** treating AI output as final authority for diagnosis, safety, or professional scope.

**Source:** Module 11 authority matrix/research verification; Module 1 scope.

# INT-09 — Knowing When the Plan Has to Change

**Modules:** 1, 4, 5, 6, 7  
**Evidence:** Critical-Domain Evidence: D2 with D1 crossover  
**Primary competency:** Modify vs. stop/refer

## Cadence primary prompt

> Imagine you have a client on the bed and the service is going well. What kinds of new information would make you change the plan — and what kinds of things would make you stop the scalp service altogether? How do you tell the difference?

## Allowed follow-up, if needed

> What if it’s just mild tenderness, the skin is intact, and you’re not seeing anything else concerning? What would you do with that?

## Rubric criteria — 10 points

1. **Can name legitimate modify signals** — comfort, pressure, regional findings, product tolerance, changing preference, etc.
2. **Can name legitimate stop/refer signals** — broken/weeping/draining skin, marked concerning change, emergency symptoms, other taught do-not-proceed findings.
3. **Does not reflexively refer every minor issue.**
4. **Does not “work around” true stop-and-refer findings because the client is comfortable.**
5. **Explains the decision threshold in terms of actual findings/context rather than a rigid script.**

**Critical Type A flags:** client comfort overriding a visible contraindication; continuing through a taught stop/refer finding; requiring pain before a visible finding is taken seriously.

**Source:** Modules 1, 4, 5, 6, 7 approved safety/service-decision frameworks.
