Telogen Effluvium: A Practitioner Education Overview

Telogen effluvium (TE) is a diffuse, temporary increase in hair shedding that reflects an exaggerated, synchronized shift of hair follicles from the growth (anagen) phase into the shedding (telogen) phase — it is part of the normal hair cycle process rather than a separate disease.

Why the distinction matters

That distinction is where a practitioner's judgment actually comes in.

Telling diffuse, temporary shedding apart from progressive, patterned hair loss matters for exactly this reason: systematic review evidence suggests some cases labeled ‘chronic’ telogen effluvium may actually be early patterned hair loss, or a trigger that's still ongoing and hasn't been identified yet.

Whether a persistent, ‘chronic’ form of telogen effluvium is really its own distinct condition is still debated in the literature — which is exactly why staying in an observation-based professional lane, rather than declaring a cause, is the right posture.

What can trigger it

That list is longer than most people expect.

Illness and inflammation, hormonal changes, psychological stress, nutritional deficiency, poor sleep, certain medications, and the postpartum period are among the documented trigger categories — no single one explains every case.

Some studies have also found lower average ferritin and vitamin D levels in people with telogen effluvium compared with people without it — one more thread of support for nutrition as a trigger category, though how strong that association is varies from study to study.

How common the shift is

This number matters because it shows the trigger effect at a population scale.

Epidemiological modeling found global TE prevalence estimates rose after the COVID-19 pandemic (from about 3.44% to 5.41% in pooled analysis), consistent with illness as a recognized trigger category.

What this page can — and cannot — tell you

Scope: This overview describes what telogen effluvium is, its relationship to the normal hair cycle, documented trigger categories, and why distinguishing it from progressive/patterned hair loss matters for practitioner observation. It does not diagnose any individual's cause of shedding, does not cover laboratory or serologic test ordering/interpretation, and does not address minoxidil dosing, prescribing, or other treatment protocols — those are separate topics outside this page's scope.

Associations between nutritional markers (e.g., vitamin D) and TE show high heterogeneity across studies, and at least one meta-analysis found the association did not reach statistical significance for TE specifically, unlike some other hair-loss conditions.

Large epidemiological reviews have not been able to separately analyze acute versus chronic TE prevalence, limiting precision about course and duration.

Some underlying sources are narrative reviews rather than systematic reviews or formal guidelines, and one practice algorithm reflects a specific national health-system context and has an associated published corrigendum.

There is no consensus definition of 'chronic' telogen effluvium, and the evidence base is mostly cross-sectional or case-series data with limited long-term follow-up, which restricts how confidently acute-versus-chronic distinctions can be drawn.

Key takeaways

    Telogen effluvium (TE) is a diffuse, temporary increase in hair shedding that reflects an exaggerated, synchronized shift of hair follicles from the growth (anagen) phase into the shedding (telogen) phase — it is part of the normal hair cycle process rather than a separate disease.

  • 02

    Distinguishing diffuse, temporary TE-type shedding from progressive, patterned hair loss is clinically relevant for practitioners making observation-based judgments, since systematic review evidence suggests some cases labeled as persistent/chronic TE may actually represent early patterned hair loss or unrecognized ongoing triggers.

  • 03
