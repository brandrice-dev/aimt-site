Sensitive Scalp: What Clients Feel and What to Observe

A client can report stinging, burning, itching, or tingling even when their scalp looks normal. A clear description of what they feel and what you can observe makes the consultation more useful. 1, 3

What people mean by sensitive scalp

An international expert group defines sensitive skin as unpleasant sensations, such as stinging, burning, itching, pain, or tingling, in response to things that would not usually provoke them. Under that definition, a skin disease does not explain the sensations. The skin can look normal or appear red. Researchers apply this definition to sensitive scalp. 1, 3

How often people report it

In a French survey of 1,011 adults, 44.2% described their scalp as sensitive or very sensitive. Other surveys use different questions and recruit different groups. Treat that figure as a report from one survey. It describes how people answered, rather than the result of a clinical exam. 2, 3

What clients say brings it on

Heat was among the triggers people reported in the French survey. A report of a trigger tells you what the person noticed around the time symptoms appeared. The study design cannot establish what caused them. 2

Ask the client to describe the sensation in their own words, tell you when it began, and explain what happened just before. Record their account alongside your observations.

What to document and when to refer

A new patch of hair loss with scale, or painful areas with persistent redness and scale around the base of hairs, warrant medical evaluation. Describe the location, appearance, and timing so the client can share those details at the appointment. 4, 5

Scope and limitations

This page covers recognition, documentation, and referral in a scalp consultation. The survey estimates are self-reported. The referral examples describe observable signs that merit medical evaluation. 2, 4, 5
