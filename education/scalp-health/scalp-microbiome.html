The Scalp Microbiome: A Practitioner Education Overview

The scalp microbiome refers to the community of bacteria and fungi that naturally live on the scalp, including bacteria such as Cutibacterium (Propionibacterium) acnes and Staphylococcus epidermidis, and fungi such as Malassezia globosa and M. restricta.

How Scientists Study the Scalp Microbiome

Before looking at what the scalp microbiome does, it helps to understand how we know about it at all.

The scalp microbiome is an active area of research, studied using modern sequencing and metagenomic methods across a range of populations and study designs.

Microbial Balance and the Concept of Dysbiosis

Flaking-type scalp presentations are conceptually linked to a shift in the balance among these microbes — often described as dysbiosis — rather than to any single organism acting alone.

This framing — balance versus imbalance — underpins much of how scalp microbiome science is discussed.

Sebum, Malassezia, and Scalp Irritation

Mechanistically, Malassezia yeasts metabolize scalp sebum, and this metabolic activity is thought to contribute to irritation and flaking.

Oiliness is part of this ecological picture: research comparing oily and non-oily, dandruff and non-dandruff scalps has observed differing microbial compositions.

Barrier Function and Microbial Ecology

Microbes don't act in isolation — they interact with the physical structure of the scalp itself.

Scalp barrier function (such as transepidermal water loss and lipid composition) and microbial ecology are conceptually interrelated, with barrier changes and microbial shifts potentially influencing one another.

The Hair Follicle as a Distinct Microbial Habitat

Scalp hair follicles form distinct, deeper niches compared to the surface skin, suggesting the follicular environment may be a separate microbial habitat worth practitioner awareness.

Why This Matters for Practitioners

None of this is about diagnosing a client — it's about understanding the ecology behind what you're observing.

What this page can — and cannot — tell you

Scope: This page is intended as a conceptual overview for beauty and scalp-care practitioners, describing general scalp microbiome composition, the balance/imbalance (dysbiosis) concept, and its conceptual relationship to flaking and oiliness and to barrier function. It does not provide diagnostic criteria, treatment protocols, medication or probiotic recommendations, or specific clinical outcome statistics, and it excludes content centered on specific diagnosed conditions (e.g., alopecia subtypes, Malassezia folliculitis, psoriasis) beyond general conceptual mention.

Causal relationships between scalp microbes and hair-follicle cycling or immune processes remain largely hypothesized rather than firmly established.

Much of the evidence comes from relatively small, observational studies that demonstrate association rather than proven cause-and-effect relationships.

Observed microbial differences between healthy and flaking scalp groups often show relatively small effect sizes, indicating host and environmental factors also play a substantial role.

Several foundational studies were conducted in specific populations or were industry-funded, which may limit how broadly the findings generalize.

Key takeaways

  • 01

    The scalp hosts a community of bacteria (such as Cutibacterium/Propionibacterium acnes and Staphylococcus epidermidis) and fungi (notably Malassezia species such as M. globosa and M. restricta), together referred to as the scalp microbiome.

  • 02

    Flaking-type scalp presentations are conceptually linked to a shift in the balance among these microbes — often called dysbiosis — rather than to any single organism acting alone.

  • 03

    Mechanistically, Malassezia yeasts metabolize scalp sebum, and this metabolic activity is thought to contribute to irritation and flaking.
