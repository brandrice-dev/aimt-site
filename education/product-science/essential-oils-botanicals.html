Essential Oils in Scalp Care: What the Research Shows

A client asks whether rosemary oil grows hair. Another asks whether a peppermint shampoo is working because it feels cool. Start by naming the result each person is asking about, then look at what the research actually tested.

Rosemary oil and hair growth

One randomized trial compared a rosemary oil preparation with 2% minoxidil in people with pattern hair loss. At six months, hair counts had increased from baseline in both groups. The study did not detect a statistically significant difference between the groups.

The trial used an active comparison and had no placebo arm. Its results leave uncertainty about how much of the observed change was due to the rosemary preparation. It also did not establish that the two products work equally well. A broader review of herbal remedies found that differences in study design and outcomes limit the conclusions researchers can draw.

Contact allergy to rosemary preparations has been documented in case reports. Those reports tell us that a reaction can happen. They do not establish how often one occurs in people using scalp products.

Peppermint oil and the cooling sensation

Menthol applied to the forearm produced a cooling sensation in a human experiment. That study measured responses on the forearm. It does not tell us whether a cooling shampoo improves the scalp finding a client came in to discuss.

One North American study reported an allergic reaction in 0.6% of the patients patch-tested for peppermint oil. These were patients referred for testing. The result does not estimate the chance of a reaction among everyone who uses a peppermint product.

Tea tree oil and dandruff research

A systematic review identified one randomized trial of tea tree oil shampoo for dandruff. In that trial, the dandruff score improved more with a shampoo containing tea tree oil than with the comparison shampoo over four weeks. The review discusses the possibility that the oil’s odor affected how well the trial stayed blinded.

That result describes the shampoo formulation used in one short trial. A review of contact allergy reports found that oxidation increased tea tree oil’s ability to cause allergic reactions and that undiluted oil was involved in most published cases. Those cases leave the risk for any given finished shampoo uncertain.

How to talk through a product claim

Ask what the client wants to understand. Are they asking about shedding, visible flaking, or the cooling sensation they noticed during a wash? Next, look at the product they brought in and ask what change they noticed and when.

Check that the evidence and the client’s question refer to the same result and product. You can use that difference to explain why one study may help answer their question while another study leaves it open.

Scope and limitations

This article describes the findings and limits of selected studies. Each section keeps the studied preparation, population, and outcome in view. The article is intended for professional education and product discussions. It does not give treatment instructions, concentrations for blending oils, or guidance for diagnosing a scalp condition.
