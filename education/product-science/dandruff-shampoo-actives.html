Dandruff Shampoo Actives: How to Read the Label

When a client asks how two dandruff shampoos differ, start with the active ingredient, its strength, and the labeled use. The Drug Facts panel gives you those details for the bottle in their hand.

Start with the product’s labeled use

Turn the bottle over and look at the active ingredient, its strength, and the Uses section. These tell you what the product contains and what it is labeled to address. Two labels can name the same active ingredient and still list different strengths or directions.

Three examples from U.S. labels

The FDA over-the-counter monograph lists selenium sulfide 1% for dandruff and seborrheic dermatitis. It also lists micronized selenium sulfide 0.6% for dandruff. Read the full ingredient name along with the strength.

Nizoral Anti-Dandruff contains ketoconazole 1%. Its label describes control of flaking, scaling, and itching associated with dandruff. The product has its own FDA application and labeling.

The cited prescription ciclopirox shampoo label lists ciclopirox 1% for scalp seborrheic dermatitis in adults. The labeled use differs from the ketoconazole 1% dandruff product above.

What the percentages can tell you

A percentage tells you the concentration of the ingredient in that product. A Cochrane review reported better clearance outcomes than inactive treatment for topical ketoconazole 2% and ciclopirox 1% in seborrheic dermatitis at about four weeks. Direct evidence for ranking antifungals was limited. The review included different formulations and application sites, and few studies followed symptoms past four weeks.

The ketoconazole result above comes from 2% treatments. The bottle in the earlier example contains 1%. Keep the study result attached to the strength and use the researchers studied.

How to compare two dandruff shampoos with a client

Put the two bottles side by side and show the client where to find the active ingredient, its strength, and the labeled use. Keep the comparison specific to those products.

The FDA monograph advises consulting a doctor if symptoms worsen or fail to improve after regular use as directed.

Scope and limitations

This article covers U.S. product labeling and the limits of evidence comparing topical antifungals. Specific label examples reflect the cited products.
